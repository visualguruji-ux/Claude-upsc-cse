Article 12 — Definition of “State”

Polity Note 01 · Part III (Fundamental Rights), Arts. 12–35
Digitised from: ForumIAS/Tapasya handwritten notes · printed coaching book p.33 · Sarrthi IAS PNP-2026 p.6
Enriched from Laxmikanth, D.D. Basu, M.P. Jain, NCERT, Vajiram, Insights, Drishti — every case web-verified against the original judgment.
⚠️ READ §10 FIRST Three of your source pages contain factual errors — two misattributed cases and one overruled case presented as current law. All are corrected in §10.

1 · Why Article 12 matters — the “gatekeeper”

  • Fundamental Rights are, as a rule, available against the State — not against private individuals.
  • Article 12 decides whom you can sue for an FR violation. It is the doorway to Part III.
  • It fixes the reach of Art. 32 (SC writ) and, indirectly, Art. 226 (HC writ).
  • Article 36 — in Part IV (DPSP), “State” has the same meaning as in Art. 12. So it governs Parts III and IV.
  • Exceptions where FRs bind private persons too: Arts. 15(2), 17, 23, 24 — and now partly 19 & 21 after Kaushal Kishor (2023) → see §8.

Syllabus hook: GS-II — Indian Constitution: features, significant provisions. Prelims — Polity & Governance.

2 · Bare text — learn it verbatim

Article 12. In this Part, unless the context otherwise requires, “the State” includes —
(a) the Government and Parliament of India,
(b) the Government and the Legislature of each of the States, and
(c) all local or other authorities
— within the territory of India or under the control of the Government of India.

The four limbs

  1. Government & Parliament of India → Union executive + legislature (President, PM, Cabinet, Lok Sabha, Rajya Sabha)
  2. Government & Legislature of each State → State executive + legislature (Governor, CM, Council of Ministers, Vidhan Sabha/Parishad)
  3. All local authorities → §4
  4. Other authorities → §5 (the heart of the topic)

Three phrases that carry marks

🔹 “includes” The definition is INCLUSIVE, not exhaustive. Courts can and do add to it.
Trap: “Article 12 exhaustively defines State” → FALSE.
🔹 “unless the context otherwise requires” Built-in flexibility — the same body may be “State” for one purpose and not another.
🔹 “within the territory of India OR under the control of the GoI” Note the disjunctive “or”. An authority outside India’s territory but under GoI control is still “State.”
Trap: “must be within Indian territory” → FALSE.

3 · Framers’ intent — Dr. Ambedkar

Constituent Assembly, 25 November 1948, on Draft Article 7. Ambedkar said the object was to make FRs binding on —

“every authority which has got either the power to make laws or the power to have discretion vested in it.”

His worry: the State acts through a vast machinery of local bodies, boards, corporations and licensing authorities. If FRs bound only the Union and the States, the State could evade them by routing action through such bodies.

➡️ This is the intellectual seed of the “instrumentality / agency” doctrine. Open a Mains answer with it — it shows you know the why, not just the case list.

4 · Limb 3 — LOCAL AUTHORITIES

4.1 The statutory anchor

The Constitution does not define “local authority.” Courts borrow Section 3(31), General Clauses Act, 1897:

“a Municipal Committee, District Board, Body of Port Commissioners or other authority legally entitled to, or entrusted by the Government with, the control or management of a municipal or local fund.”

⭐ The bridge — worth a mark on its own Article 367(1) makes the General Clauses Act applicable to the interpretation of the Constitution. This single line lifts a Mains answer above coaching-note level.

4.2 THE case — Union of India v. R.C. Jain (1981), SC

Facts: Was the Delhi Development Authority (DDA) a “local authority” under s. 32(iv), Payment of Bonus Act, 1965?

Held: YES — DDA is a local authority. The Court laid down five attributes.

🧠 Mnemonic: L · A · A · F · S
L
separate Legal existence
Must be a body corporate, legally independent — not a mere government agency/department.
A
functions in a defined Area
…and must “ordinarily, wholly or partly, directly or indirectly, be elected by the inhabitants” of that area.
⚠️ The nuance your note drops The Court said “ordinarily” → election is a strong indicator, NOT a mandatory condition. That is exactly why the non-elected DDA still qualified.
Prelims trap: “An elected body is essential for local-authority status” → FALSE.
A
Autonomy
An “appreciable measure of autonomy” — freedom to decide policy for its area. Need not be complete.
F
power to raise Funds
By levying taxes, rates, charges or fees. May be in addition to grants/borrowings.
Essential: control or management of the fund must vest in the authority itself.
S
entrusted by Statute with municipal-type functions
Health & education, water & sewerage, town planning & development, roads, markets, transport, social welfare.

4.3 Examples

Municipalities · Municipal Corporations · Panchayats · District Boards · Improvement Trusts · Port Commissioners · Notified Area / Town Area Committees · Development Authorities (DDA) · Mining Boards

5 · Limb 4 — “OTHER AUTHORITIES” (the heart)

Nearly all the litigation — and all the exam questions — sit here. Learn it as four phases.

PHASE 1 — Narrow / restrictive view (now REJECTED)

University of Madras v. Shantha Bai Madras HIGH COURT · 1 May 1953 · AIR 1954 Mad 67

Facts: MGM College, Udipi (affiliated to Madras University) was allowed only 10 girl students in Junior Intermediate; the Syndicate directed that no women be admitted in future without special sanction. Shantha Bai was refused admission in 1951 and challenged it.

Held on Art. 12:

“These words must be construed ejusdem generis with Government or Legislature and so construed can only mean authorities exercising governmental functions. They would not include persons natural or juristic who cannot be regarded as instrumentalities of the Government.”

University of Madras — a body corporate under Madras Act VII of 1923, aided but not maintained by the State, existing only to promote education → NOT “State.”

Second limb: Art. 29(2) — the specific admissions provision — omits “sex” as a ground; applying generalia specialibus non derogant, 29(2) prevailed over the general Art. 15(1).

Outcome: the University WON. Shantha Bai lost.

🚨 This reasoning is DEAD See Phase 2. Cite Santa Bai only as the starting point of the restrictive era — never as current law.

PHASE 2 — Ejusdem generis rejected

Ujjam Bai v. State of UP1962 · Supreme Court

Ayyangar J.: “other authorities” cannot be read ejusdem generis with either the Government/Legislatures or local authorities; the words are of wide amplitude, covering every authority created under a statute.

⭐ Rajasthan State Electricity Board v. Mohan Lal — the clincher1967 · Supreme Court
  • Ejusdem generis cannot apply because there is NO COMMON GENUS running through the bodies listed in Art. 12.
  • “Other authorities” = all authorities created by the Constitution or by statute on whom powers are conferred by law.
  • It is NOT necessary that the body perform governmental or sovereign functions — it may even be engaged in commercial activity.
  • RSEB held to be “State.”

PHASE 3 — The AGENCY / INSTRUMENTALITY doctrine

Sukhdev Singh v. Bhagatram1975 · SC Statutory corporations ONGC, LIC, IFCI are “State”; their regulations have the force of law. (Mathew J.’s concurrence seeded the instrumentality idea.)
R.D. Shetty v. International Airport Authority of India1979 · SC · Bhagwati J. The original 5-point test. Key insight: the corporate veil is irrelevant — the State cannot escape FRs by acting through a corporation.
Som Prakash Rekhi v. Union of India1981 · SC A government company (Bharat Petroleum) is “State”.
⭐ Ajay Hasia v. Khalid Mujib Sehravardi1981 · (1981) 1 SCC 722 · AIR 1981 SC 487 Consolidated Shetty’s five into the canonical SIX TESTS. A registered society running REC Srinagar was held to be State.
⭐ The 6 Ajay Hasia tests (verbatim)
  1. Whether the share-capital of the corporation is held by the Government
  2. Whether the financial assistance of the State meets almost the entire expenditure
  3. Whether the corporation enjoys a State-conferred or State-protected monopoly status
  4. Whether there is deep and pervasive State control
  5. Whether the functions are of public importance and closely related to governmental functions
  6. Whether a department of Government has been transferred to the corporation
🧠 Mnemonic: S-F-M-C-F-D → Share capital · Financial assistance · Monopoly · Control (deep & pervasive) · Functions of public importance · Department transferred
Grouping (advanced — good for Mains) Tests 1, 2, 4 → measure government ownership/control. Tests 3, 5, 6 → functional tests.
⭐ Ratio: form is irrelevant — substance of control decides. A society, a company, even a private body can be “State”.

PHASE 4 — The FINAL, authoritative position

⭐⭐ Pradeep Kumar Biswas v. Indian Institute of Chemical Biology 16 April 2002 · (2002) 5 SCC 111 · SEVEN-judge Constitution Bench · 5:2 · majority by Ruma Pal J.
  • Held: CSIR IS “State” under Article 12.
  • OVERRULED Sabhajit Tewary v. Union of India (1975) — a 5-judge bench that had held CSIR not to be State. (That is precisely why a 7-judge bench was constituted.)
  • The test: the body must be financially, functionally and administratively dominated by, or under the control of, the Government — and that control must be “particular to the body” and “deep and pervasive.”
  • ⭐ Mere government patronage, regulation or financial assistance is NOT enough. The enquiry is functional, not formal — decided case by case on cumulative facts.

Why it is “the final judgement” (as your handwritten note correctly says): largest bench, it settled the conflict, and every later case applies its formula.

6 · Is the JUDICIARY “State”?

❌ NOT “State” — when exercising JUDICIAL functions (hearing cases, delivering judgments, passing judicial orders)

Rationale: to preserve judicial independence — a judicial decision cannot itself be attacked as an FR violation, else litigation would never end.
  • Naresh Shridhar Mirajkar v. State of Maharashtra (1966) — 9-judge bench: a judicial order of the High Court cannot be challenged under Art. 32. The remedy is appeal, not a writ.
  • Rupa Ashok Hurra v. Ashok Hurra (2002) — reaffirmed that superior courts exercising judicial functions are not “State”/“other authorities”, and judicial orders cannot be challenged for violating FRs. (This is also the case that created the CURATIVE PETITION — high-value add-on.)
✅ IS “State” — when exercising ADMINISTRATIVE / RULE-MAKING functions Appointments to court staff, service matters, framing rules under Arts. 145/227, administrative decisions. Here the court acts like any other authority and is bound by Part III.
🧠 “Judging → NOT State. Managing / Rule-making → State.”
⚡ Advanced nuance (Mains / interview — beyond prelims) A.R. Antulay v. R.S. Nayak (1988), 7-judge bench sits uneasily with Mirajkar. There the SC held that its own earlier direction transferring a trial was without jurisdiction and violated Arts. 14 & 21, and recalled it. So the SC has in substance accepted that a judicial order can infringe FRs — even if the remedy is recall/review/curative rather than an Art. 32 writ.

Coaching material flattens this. Don’t cite Antulay as simply “rule-making = State” — cite it as the exception showing judicial orders are not wholly immune.

7 · SCORECARD — who is “State”, who is not

✅ Held to BE “State”

  • Rajasthan State Electricity Board — RSEB v. Mohan Lal (1967)
  • ONGC, LIC, IFCI — Sukhdev Singh (1975)
  • International Airport Authority — R.D. Shetty (1979)
  • Society running REC Srinagar — Ajay Hasia (1981)
  • Bharat Petroleum (govt. company) — Som Prakash Rekhi (1981)
  • Delhi Development Authority (as a local authority) — R.C. Jain (1981)
  • CSIR — Pradeep Kumar Biswas (2002) ⭐
  • SAIL and other PSUs meeting the tests
  • Municipalities, Panchayats, District Boards, Improvement Trusts, Port Commissioners

❌ Held NOT to be “State”

  • University of Madras — Santa Bai (1954, Mad HC) — reasoning since rejected
  • CSIR — Sabhajit Tewary (1975) ⚠️ OVERRULED in 2002
  • NCERT — Chander Mohan Khanna v. NCERT (1991)
  • BCCI — Zee Telefilms v. Union of India (2005)
  • Judiciary, when performing judicial functions

The two “not State” cases your pages highlight

Chander Mohan Khanna v. NCERT17 September 1991 · SC

Employee’s termination challenged; NCERT objected that it is not amenable to writ jurisdiction.

Held: NCERT is NOT “State.” It is “largely an autonomous body”; its activities are not wholly related to governmental functions; government control is confined only to proper utilisation of the grant; its funds include grants plus contributions from other sources plus income from its own assets, which it is free to apply to its own objectives.

⭐ “Article 12 should not be stretched so as to bring in every autonomous body which has some nexus with the Government within the sweep of the expression ‘State’. A wide enlargement of the meaning must be tempered by a wise limitation.”

Zee Telefilms Ltd. v. Union of India2005 · (2005) 4 SCC 649 · 5-judge bench · 3:2

Held: BCCI is NOT “State” under Art. 12 → no Art. 32 remedy.

Reasons: not created by any statute; no share capital held by government; no substantial financial assistance from government; its monopoly over cricket administration is neither conferred nor protected by the State (it is de facto, not de jure); no deep and pervasive control.

⭐ The crucial second half your book omits Because BCCI discharges public functions (selecting the national team, regulating the sport), an aggrieved person CAN move the High Court under Article 226.

Therefore: Art. 226 is WIDER than Art. 32. Art. 32 lies only against “State”; Art. 226 lies additionally against a private body performing a public duty — Andi Mukta Sadguru Trust v. V.R. Rudani (1989).

Current-affairs link: BCCI v. Cricket Association of Bihar (2015) → SC affirmed BCCI’s public functions and appointed the Justice R.M. Lodha Committee; reforms later folded into BCCI’s constitution.

8 · Private bodies, public functions & HORIZONTAL rights

General rule: private bodies are not automatically “State”.

Three routes by which a private entity can still be reached:

  1. Instrumentality / agency → it is State (Ajay Hasia / Pradeep Kumar Biswas tests)
  2. Deep and pervasive State control over functioning, finance and policy
  3. Public function test → not State, but still amenable to an Art. 226 writ (Zee Telefilms; Andi Mukta)
🔥 THE BIG RECENT SHIFT — Kaushal Kishor v. State of UP (3 Jan 2023) 5-judge Constitution Bench · 4:1
  • Held that Fundamental Rights under Articles 19 and 21 can be enforced even against PRIVATE / NON-STATE ACTORS — i.e. Arts. 19 and 21 are, to that extent, HORIZONTAL.
  • Also held: the grounds in Art. 19(2) are exhaustive — no additional restrictions can be read in.
  • Significance here: it dilutes Article 12’s gatekeeping role. The old binary (“State → FRs apply; private → they don’t”) is breaking down.
This is the single best value-addition for a Mains answer or interview on Article 12. Very few aspirants link it.

Pre-existing horizontal FRs (available against private persons too)

  • Art. 15(2) — no denial of access to shops, hotels, public restaurants, wells, tanks, roads
  • Art. 17 — abolition of untouchability
  • Art. 23 — traffic in human beings, begar, forced labour
  • Art. 24 — no child below 14 in factories/mines/hazardous work

Note: Arts. 17, 23, 24 are exactly the ones in the table at the top of your first page — that table is the horizontal-rights set. Good linkage to make.

9 · Linkages — the articles around Art. 12

Article 13 — where your Sarrthi page goes next
  • 13(1): pre-Constitution laws inconsistent with Part III are void to the extent of inconsistency
  • 13(2): the State shall not make any law taking away/abridging FRs
  • ⭐ 13(3)(a): “law” includes any ordinance, order, bye-law, rule, regulation, notification, custom or usage having the force of law
  • 13(4): a constitutional amendment under Art. 368 is not “law” (24th Amendment; read with Kesavananda Bharati, 1973 — amendments still testable on the basic structure doctrine)
  • Doctrines: Severability · Eclipse · Waiver (FRs cannot be waived — Basheshar Nath v. CIT)
The bridge: Mohammad Yasin struck down a Town Area Committee’s bye-laws — possible only because the Committee is “State” under Art. 12 and its bye-laws are “law” under Art. 13(3)(a).
  • Article 32 — writ against “State” only (a FR itself; Ambedkar’s “heart and soul of the Constitution”)
  • Article 226 — wider: any legal right + against private bodies performing public duties
  • Article 36 — “State” in Part IV = same meaning as Art. 12
  • Article 367(1) — General Clauses Act applies to constitutional interpretation

10 · 🚨 CORRECTIONS TO YOUR SOURCE PAGES

❌ ERROR 1 — printed book, p.33 “In Mohammad Yasin vs Town Area Committee, the Supreme Court gave four criteria for an entity to be classified as a ‘local authority’ under Article 12.”

WRONG. Mohammad Yasin v. Town Area Committee, Jalalabad (AIR 1952 SC 115, 27 Feb 1952) is an Article 19(1)(g) / Article 32 case. It lays down no test for “local authority” anywhere in the judgment.
  • Its actual ratio: a Town Area Committee’s bye-laws levying a licence fee (one anna per rupee) on wholesale fruit/vegetable dealers irrespective of use of Committee property were ultra vires the U.P. Municipalities Act; “a licence fee on a business… operates as a restriction on his right to carry on his business” under Art. 19(1)(g), not saved by 19(6). Art. 265 also invoked.
  • The correct case is Union of India v. R.C. Jain (1981) — the LAAFS five attributes. Your handwritten ForumIAS note is right.
  • Where the book’s “four criteria” actually come from: a garbled import from the “other authorities” line — (a),(b),(c) from Rajasthan SEB v. Mohan Lal (1967), and (d) “substantially financed by government” from the Ajay Hasia / Pradeep Kumar Biswas instrumentality tests. The book has merged the two branches of Article 12.
❌ ERROR 2 — printed book, p.33 University of Madras v. Santa Bai (1954) is presented as current law.

Its ejusdem generis reasoning was expressly rejected by the Supreme Court in Ujjam Bai (1962) and buried in Rajasthan SEB v. Mohan Lal (1967) (“no common genus”). It is a High Court decision and is no longer good law.
  • Also: the book’s phrase “unless it is maintained by the State” is not part of the Article 12 test at all — that is the language of Article 29(2) (“maintained by the State or receiving aid out of State funds”), used in the separate discrimination limb. The book has welded a bit of Art. 29(2) onto the Art. 12 holding.
  • Irony worth remembering: under Santa Bai a university was not State; under Ajay Hasia / Pradeep Kumar Biswas, universities, societies and even private bodies routinely are — because control and funding matter, not “sovereign functions”.
❌ ERROR 3 — Sarrthi IAS PNP-2026, p.6 “In Rati Lal v. State of Bombay — the judiciary is not state for the purpose of Article 12.”

Unsupported. Ratilal Panachand Gandhi v. State of Bombay (1954) is a case on the Bombay Public Trusts Act, 1950 and Articles 25 & 26 (freedom of religion, rights of religious denominations). The judgment does not discuss Article 12 or the judiciary’s status at all. This citation is copied through secondary material.

Use instead: Naresh Shridhar Mirajkar (1966) and Rupa Ashok Hurra (2002).
⚠️ MINOR — Sarrthi The case is Naresh Shridhar Mirajkar, not “N.S. Mirajkar” — write the full name in Mains. And see §6 for why A.R. Antulay should not be cited as a simple “rule-making = State” authority.
✅ WHAT YOUR HANDWRITTEN NOTES GET RIGHT Both the red LAAFS box and the green Ajay Hasia page: the two-branch split, LAAFS, R.D. Shetty → Ajay Hasia 6 parameters → Pradeep Kumar Biswas as final, the judiciary dual position, and NCERT ≠ State / BCCI ≠ State.

12 · Mains angle (GS-II)

Likely framings

  1. “The expression ‘other authorities’ in Article 12 has been judicially expanded far beyond the framers’ contemplation.” Examine.
  2. “Article 12 is the gatekeeper of Part III. In an era of privatisation and PPP, is that gate too narrow?” Critically comment.
  3. “Should the judiciary be brought within the definition of ‘State’ under Article 12?” Discuss.

Skeleton for Q1 / Q2

  • Intro: Ambedkar’s intent — FRs must bind “every authority which has… the power to have discretion vested in it” — quote it.
  • Body — the arc: restrictive (Santa Bai, ejusdem generis) → rejection (Ujjam Bai, Rajasthan SEB) → instrumentality (Shetty, Ajay Hasia) → consolidation and a deliberate brake (Pradeep Kumar Biswas; NCERT — “a wide enlargement must be tempered by a wise limitation”).
  • The contemporary problem: liberalisation, disinvestment, PPP, outsourced public services — private hospitals, private schools, toll roads, platform economy → State functions migrate to private hands, outside Art. 12.
  • The judicial answer so far: the public function test under Art. 226 (Zee Telefilms, Andi Mukta) and now horizontality under Kaushal Kishor (2023).
  • Balance: over-expansion burdens genuinely autonomous institutions and chills private enterprise (the NCERT caution); under-inclusion lets the State evade FRs by outsourcing.
  • Conclusion: the trend is from an institutional/formal test to a functional one — what is being done, not who is doing it. Suggest a function-based reading consistent with Ambedkar’s purpose.
💎 Value-addition arsenal
  • Ambedkar’s CAD quote (25 Nov 1948)
  • NCERT: the “wise limitation” line
  • Pradeep Kumar Biswas: “financially, functionally, administratively dominated”
  • Kaushal Kishor (2023): horizontality
  • Comparative: US “state action” doctrine; South Africa — Art. 8(2) of its Constitution expressly binds private persons to the Bill of Rights
  • 2nd ARC + Art. 226 public-function jurisprudence on accountability of outsourced public services

13 · PYQ signal

  • UPSC Prelims: Art. 12 is rarely a standalone MCQ; it surfaces indirectly — through against whom FRs are enforceable, Art. 32 vs 226, and which bodies are amenable to writs. Prepare the traps in §11 rather than case-year matching.
  • UPSC Mains (GS-II): appears through enforceability of rights against non-State actors, judicial review, and accountability of PSUs/autonomous bodies.
  • State PSCs (UPPSC, BPSC, MPPSC, RPSC): these do ask direct case-name ↔ holding matching (RSEB, Ajay Hasia, Pradeep Kumar Biswas, Zee Telefilms). The §7 scorecard and §11 chronology are built for exactly that.

14 · Practice MCQs (self-made — not PYQs)

Q1. Consider the following statements regarding Article 12:
  1. The definition of “State” given in Article 12 is exhaustive.
  2. An authority located outside the territory of India cannot be “State” even if it is under the control of the Government of India.
  3. “State” in Article 12 has the same meaning for the Directive Principles.
Which are correct?
  • (a) 1 and 2
  • (b) 3 only
  • (c) 2 and 3
  • (d) 1, 2 and 3
Answer(b) 3 only. 1 is wrong — “includes” makes it inclusive. 2 is wrong — the “or” is disjunctive. 3 is right — Article 36.
Q2. In which case did the Supreme Court hold that “other authorities” cannot be read ejusdem generis with the preceding expressions in Article 12?
  • (a) University of Madras v. Santa Bai
  • (b) Rajasthan State Electricity Board v. Mohan Lal
  • (c) Mohammad Yasin v. Town Area Committee
  • (d) Union of India v. R.C. Jain
Answer(b). Because there is no common genus among the bodies listed. Ujjam Bai (1962) said so earlier; RSEB (1967) settled it. (a) held the opposite and is no longer good law.
Q3. Which of the following has been held NOT to be “State” under Article 12?
  1. CSIR
  2. NCERT
  3. BCCI
  4. Rajasthan State Electricity Board
  • (a) 1 and 2
  • (b) 2 and 3
  • (c) 1, 2 and 3
  • (d) 3 and 4
Answer(b) 2 and 3. CSIR is State (Pradeep Kumar Biswas, 2002 — overruling Sabhajit Tewary, 1975 — a favourite trap). RSEB is State (1967).
Q4. Regarding the judiciary and Article 12, which is correct?
  • (a) The judiciary is “State” for all purposes
  • (b) The judiciary is never “State”
  • (c) The judiciary is “State” when exercising administrative and rule-making functions, but not when exercising judicial functions
  • (d) Only subordinate courts are “State”
Answer(c). Naresh Shridhar Mirajkar (1966), reaffirmed in Rupa Ashok Hurra (2002).
Q5. Consider:
  1. BCCI is “State” under Article 12 and hence a writ under Article 32 lies against it.
  2. A private body performing a public duty may be subject to a writ under Article 226.
Which is/are correct?
  • (a) 1 only
  • (b) 2 only
  • (c) Both
  • (d) Neither
Answer(b) 2 only. Zee Telefilms (2005): BCCI is not State → no Art. 32; but Art. 226 lies for its public functions. Art. 226 is wider than Art. 32.
