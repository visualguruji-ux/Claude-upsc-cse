## PART A — SCOPE OF THE SUBJECT & COMPARISON WITH OTHER SOCIAL SCIENCES

### A.1 What is Sociology? (etymology + definitions)
- **Etymology:** *socius* (Latin = companion/associate) + *logos* (Greek = study/science) → "science of society / companionship."
- **Coined by Auguste Comte** in **1839** (Vol. IV of *Positive Philosophy*). He first called it **"social physics"**, but dropped it after the Belgian statistician **Adolphe Quetelet** used that term — and adopted **"sociology."**

**Classic definitions (learn 3–4 verbatim):**
| Thinker | Definition (essence) |
|---|---|
| **Comte** | Science of **social order (statics)** and **social progress (dynamics)**. |
| **Durkheim** | Science of **social institutions** — their genesis & functioning; the study of **social facts**. |
| **Max Weber** | Science that attempts the **interpretive understanding (*Verstehen*) of social action**. |
| **Morris Ginsberg** | Study of **human interactions and inter-relations**, their conditions and consequences. |
| **Kingsley Davis / Kimball Young** | **General science of society** / study of social groups. |

### A.2 Scope of Sociology — the two schools
There is **no consensus** on the scope of sociology. Two rival schools:

**(i) Formalistic / Specialistic School** — *Georg Simmel* (leader), Vierkandt, Von Wiese, Tönnies, Max Weber, Small.
- Sociology should be a **pure, independent, specific science with a limited scope.**
- It studies the **FORMS** of social relationships, **abstracted from their CONTENTS.**
  - **Simmel:** distinguish *forms* (competition, cooperation, subordination, super-ordination, division of labour) from *contents* (economic, political, religious). Sociology studies the recurring forms.
  - **Von Wiese:** forms = associative + dissociative processes.
  - **Vierkandt:** ultimate forms of mental/psychic relationships.
  - **Weber:** sociology = interpretive understanding of particular forms of social behaviour.
- **Criticism:** form/content cannot be neatly separated; abstraction of pure forms is unreal; other sciences (geometry, economics, international law) also study forms; conception too narrow.

**(ii) Synthetic School** — *Émile Durkheim, L.T. Hobhouse, Morris Ginsberg, P.A. Sorokin.*
- Sociology is a **general, synthesising science** — broad, encyclopaedic; a **synthesis of the social sciences.**
  - **Durkheim's division of sociology into 3 parts:**
    1. **Social Morphology** – geographical/territorial substratum; population (size, density, distribution).
    2. **Social Physiology** – dynamic branches: sociology of religion, morals, law, economic life.
    3. **General Sociology** – philosophical part; general character of social facts; formulation of general social laws.
  - **Ginsberg:** four tasks — social morphology, social control, social processes, social pathology.
  - **Sorokin:** relationships & correlations between different classes of social phenomena + their common characteristics.
- **Criticism:** if sociology synthesises everything it risks losing a distinct identity.

> **Balanced view (exam conclusion):** Sociology is best seen as a **general science of society with its own perspective** — it studies society *as a whole and as a system of relationships*, while other social sciences study *particular segments*. Its scope is therefore **wide but distinctive.**

### A.3 Nature of Sociology (Robert Bierstedt — quick list)
Independent · a **social** (not natural) science · **categorical** ("what is") not **normative** ("what ought") · **pure** not applied · relatively **abstract** not concrete · **generalising** not particularising · a **general** social science · **both rational and empirical.**

### A.4 Comparison with Other Social Sciences

**Sociology & Anthropology** — *"twin sisters"* (Kroeber); Evans-Pritchard, A.R. Radcliffe-Brown, M.N. Srinivas bridge both.
- *Convergence:* both study human society & culture; social/cultural anthropology ≈ sociology in method & concept.
- *Divergence:* Anthropology → **small, simple, pre-literate, homogeneous, "primitive"/tribal** societies studied **holistically** via **fieldwork/participant observation/ethnography**; Sociology → **large, complex, modern, literate, heterogeneous** societies studied via **surveys, statistics, mixed methods.** (Distinction is fading — e.g., **Srinivas' village studies** are both.)

**Sociology & Political Science**
- PS = study of the **State, government, power, formal political organisation** (man as *political* being).
- Sociology = **all** social relationships including political (man as *social* being).
- *Overlap:* birth of **Political Sociology** (power, authority, elites, political socialisation).
- *Divergence:* PS studies only **organised/state** societies & political relations; sociology studies **all** societies & relations; PS narrower & more concrete.

**Sociology & Economics**
- Economics = **production, distribution, exchange, consumption** of goods/services — **one specialised aspect** of social life (man as *economic* being).
- Economics is **precise, quantitative, uses `ceteris paribus`**; sociology treats economic life as **embedded in social life.**
- *Overlap:* **Economic Sociology** — Weber's *Protestant Ethic & the Spirit of Capitalism*, Marx's economic base–superstructure, Veblen's *conspicuous consumption*, Polanyi's *embeddedness*.
- *Divergence:* Economics = **narrow & deep**; Sociology = **broad**; economics more concrete/measurable.

**Sociology & History**
- History = record of the **concrete, unique past events** in chronological order → **particularising / idiographic.**
- Sociology = **recurrent patterns, abstract, general laws** → **generalising / nomothetic.**
- **G.E. Howard:** *"History is past sociology; sociology is present history."*
- *Overlap:* **Historical Sociology** (Weber, Marx, Barrington Moore); history gives raw data, sociology gives the framework.

**Sociology & Psychology**
- Psychology = science of the **individual mind & behaviour**; Sociology = **group/collective/social behaviour.**
- *Bridge:* **Social Psychology** — the individual *in* the social context (G.H. Mead, attitudes, socialisation).
- Durkheim insisted **social facts** must be explained by **other social facts, not by individual psychology** ("sui generis").

> **One-liner memory hook:** *Economics, Political Science, History* study **slices** of social reality; **Sociology studies the whole loaf** — and *how the slices interconnect.*

## PART B — SCIENCE, SCIENTIFIC METHOD AND CRITIQUE

### B.1 What is "Science"? Two meanings
1. A **body of systematic, verified knowledge**, and
2. A **method/approach** to acquiring that knowledge.

**Attributes of the scientific method:** empiricism (observation) · objectivity · value-neutrality (ethical neutrality) · verifiability/replication · cause–effect relations · generalisation · prediction · precision/quantification · reliability & validity · systematic procedure.

**Steps in the scientific method (cycle):**
`Observation → Hypothesis → Data collection → Analysis/testing → Generalisation/theory → Prediction → Verification/falsification` → (back to observation).

### B.2 The case FOR Sociology as a Science (Positivist tradition)
- **Auguste Comte — Positivism:** society is governed by **invariable natural laws** discoverable through observation, comparison, experiment. **Law of Three Stages** — knowledge evolves Theological → Metaphysical → **Positive (scientific).** Sociology = the "queen of the sciences."
- **Émile Durkheim — the great demonstrator:**
  - *The Rules of Sociological Method* (1895): treat **social facts as things (choses)** — they are **external**, **exercise constraint**, and are **general/collective**; study them **objectively.**
  - *Suicide* (1897): a landmark of **scientific sociology** — used **official statistics**, the **comparative method**, and **correlation** to link suicide rates to **social integration & regulation** (egoistic, altruistic, anomic, fatalistic). Explained a "psychological" act by **social facts.**
- Sociology **does** use scientific tools: observation (participant/non-participant), the comparative method, historical method, surveys, sample studies, (limited) experiments, and statistics.

### B.3 The CRITIQUE — why sociology is *not exactly* like natural sciences
1. **Objectivity is hard** — the sociologist is *inside* the society being studied; carries values, ideology, class, gender, cultural bias.
2. **Experimentation is limited** — no controlled lab; ethical & practical limits; humans are active, not passive objects.
3. **Prediction is weak** — human free will, reflexivity and complexity make precise prediction difficult; **self-fulfilling / self-defeating prophecies** (Merton).
4. **Generalisation is difficult** — societies vary across time, space, culture; universal laws are elusive.
5. **Measurement/quantification problems** — attitudes, values, meanings have no natural units.
6. **The Hawthorne Effect** — subjects change behaviour when observed → contaminates data.
7. **Distinct subject matter** — humans possess **consciousness, meaning and purpose** (unlike atoms) → studying them "as things" misses the point (Weber's key objection).

### B.4 The Interpretive / Anti-Positivist alternative
- **Max Weber — Interpretive Sociology (*Verstehen*):** social action carries **subjective meaning**; sociology must **interpret** the meanings actors attach to action, and combine this with **causal explanation** (*Erklären*). Sociology **is** a science — but a **special social science** with its own method. Tools: **Ideal type**, **value-relevance** in choosing problems + **value-freedom (*Wertfreiheit*)** in analysis.
- **Non-positivist methodologies (syllabus link):**
  - **Phenomenology** — Alfred Schutz (life-world, typifications).
  - **Ethnomethodology** — Harold Garfinkel (how members produce everyday order; "breaching experiments").
  - **Symbolic Interactionism** — G.H. Mead, Herbert Blumer (meaning arises in interaction).
  - **Social Constructionism** — Berger & Luckmann, *The Social Construction of Reality* (1966).
- **Anti-naturalism:** society is *fundamentally different* from nature → natural-science method is inappropriate.

### B.5 Post-Positivist & critical challenges (value-addition)
- **Karl Popper — Falsificationism:** science advances by **attempting to falsify**, not verify; critique of induction. Puts pressure on "verificationist" positivism.
- **Thomas Kuhn — Paradigms:** *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions* (1962) — science is **paradigm-bound** and shaped by community consensus, not purely objective/cumulative; sociology is **"multi-paradigmatic / pre-paradigmatic."**
- **Frankfurt School / Critical Theory** — Adorno, Horkheimer, **Habermas**: positivism is not neutral — it **serves the status quo**; Habermas' **three knowledge-interests** (technical, practical-hermeneutic, emancipatory).
- **Feminist critique** — mainstream sociology is *"malestream"*; positivist "objectivity" is masculinist; **Standpoint theory** (Dorothy Smith, Sandra Harding).
- **Postmodernism** (Lyotard, Foucault) — rejects grand narratives and universal scientific truth.

### B.6 Fact, Value & Objectivity debate (recurring PYQ)
- **Weber:** research should be **value-free (*Wertfreiheit*)** in analysis, but **value-relevant** in the *selection* of the problem. → *Objectivity* = following rigorous method free of personal bias; *value-neutrality* = not letting one's own value-preferences distort findings.
- **Gunnar Myrdal:** a completely value-free sociology is a **myth**; the honest path is to **state your value-premises explicitly.**
- **Alvin Gouldner:** *"Anti-Minotaur: The Myth of a Value-Free Sociology"* — value-freedom itself became an ideology.

### B.7 Verdict (use as conclusion)
> Sociology **is a science** — it employs systematic, empirical, self-correcting methods and strives for objectivity — **but it is a *social* science, not a carbon copy of physics.** Its subject matter (conscious, meaning-making human beings) demands **both** causal **explanation** *and* interpretive **understanding**. It is **multi-paradigmatic**, and its "objectivity" is a disciplined aspiration secured through method, peer scrutiny and reflexivity — not the value-free certainty of the natural sciences.
