# 1. THE MODERATES (Early Nationalists), 1885-1905

Also called the **early nationalists** or **liberals**. They dominated the Congress in its
first two decades.

## 1.1 Prominent leaders (and why each matters)
- **Dadabhai Naoroji** - the "Grand Old Man of India"; author of the **Drain of Wealth** theory;
  Congress president 3 times; the **first Indian to become a British MP** (1892, Liberal).
- **Surendranath Banerjee** - founder of the Indian Association; the "Indian Burke" / "Rashtraguru".
- **Pherozeshah Mehta** - the "Lion of Bombay".
- **Gopal Krishna Gokhale** - founded the **Servants of India Society (1905)**; **Gandhi's
  political guru**.
- **W.C. Bonnerjee** - the Congress's first president (1885).
- **Badruddin Tyabji** - the first Muslim president of the Congress (1887).
- Others: **Dinshaw Wacha, M.G. Ranade, R.C. Dutt, Anand Mohan Bose, Madan Mohan Malaviya,
  William Wedderburn** (a British ally).

## 1.2 Ideology and beliefs
- **Faith in the British sense of justice** and in the **"providential mission"** of British
  rule (they believed the connection with Britain could be reformed to India's benefit).
- **Gradualism and constitutionalism** - reform step by step, within the system.
- Believed India needed a period of **political apprenticeship**; loyal to the Crown.
- Deeply influenced by **Western liberal and democratic thought** (Mill, Burke, the French and
  American revolutions).

## 1.3 Methods
- **Constitutional agitation "within the four corners of the law"** (Bipan Chandra's phrase).
- The **"3 P's": Petition, Prayer, Protest** - petitions, memorials, resolutions, public
  meetings, deputations and press articles. (Some coaching materials add a **4th P,
  "Propaganda"** - i.e., political education.)
- A **two-pronged strategy:**
  1. Build a **strong, united public opinion in India** and educate people politically.
  2. **Persuade the British** Government and public to introduce reforms - through the
     **British Committee of the INC (1889)** and its journal **"India"**, and through
     **Naoroji's presence as an MP** in the House of Commons.
- Their reliance on prayers and petitions was later mocked by the Extremists as
  **"political mendicancy"** (the politics of begging).

## 1.4 The economic critique - "Economic Nationalism" (their greatest contribution)
The Moderates produced the most powerful **economic critique of colonialism** - the idea that
British rule was the cause of India's poverty.
- **Dadabhai Naoroji** - "**Poverty and Un-British Rule in India**" (1901); developed the
  **Drain of Wealth** theory.
- **R.C. Dutt** - "**The Economic History of India**", made the drain its central theme.
- **William Digby** - "**Prosperous British India**".
- **The "drain"** - a one-way transfer of India's wealth to Britain through: the **Home
  Charges**, salaries and pensions of British officials, interest on the public debt, guaranteed
  profits to British railway/investment, and military expenditure.
- **The three pillars of the critique:** (1) **de-industrialisation** (ruin of handicrafts by
  free-trade/British goods); (2) the **drain of wealth**; (3) an **exploitative land-revenue
  system** and heavy taxation.
- **Demands flowing from it:** Indianisation of services (to keep salaries in India), reduction
  of military/"Home Charges" spending, **protective tariffs** for Indian industry, lower land
  revenue, and development of modern industry.

## 1.5 Political and administrative demands
- **Expansion and reform of the legislative councils** - more elected Indian members and
  **control over the budget** ("no taxation without representation").
- **Separation of the judiciary from the executive.**
- **Indianisation of the civil service** - holding the **ICS examination simultaneously in
  India and England** and **raising the age limit**.
- **Repeal of the Arms Act**; extension of civil liberties (speech, press, association).
- More spending on **primary and technical education**.

## 1.6 Achievements
- **Created a national awakening** - a sense of belonging to one nation - and trained Indians in
  modern politics.
- The **economic critique undermined the moral legitimacy** of British rule (Bipan Chandra rates
  this as their single greatest achievement).
- Secured the **Indian Councils Act 1892** (enlarged councils, budget discussion, questions).
- **Public Service Commission (1886)**; a **House of Commons resolution (1893)** for simultaneous
  ICS exams; the **Welby Commission on Indian Expenditure (1895)**, with Naoroji as a member.
- **Popularised** the ideas of democracy, civil liberties, representative government and
  secular nationalism.

## 1.7 Evaluation (Bipan Chandra's assessment)
- **Limitations:** a **narrow social base** (urban educated professionals); they **did not draw
  in the masses** and "lacked faith" in them; they overestimated British liberalism; and results
  were slow, which frustrated the younger generation.
- **But they were pioneers, not failures:** they built the **organisation, the ideology and the
  economic critique** on which every later phase of the movement stood. As Bipan Chandra argues,
  the early nationalists laid the **firm foundations** of the national movement - their real
  contribution was the **political and economic education of a nation**.

# 2. THE EXTREMISTS (Assertive Nationalists), 1905-1919

Also called the **Garam Dal**, the assertive nationalists, or the neo-nationalists.

## 2.1 Causes of the rise of Extremism
1. **Realisation of the true (exploitative) nature of British rule** - the drain theory exposed
   it, reinforced by **famines (1896-97, 1899-1900)** and the **plague (1896-97)**.
2. **Failure of the Moderate methods** - petitions were ignored; British hostility (e.g., the
   Ilbert Bill episode) showed that reform would not come by prayer.
3. **Growth of self-respect and self-confidence**, and the leadership of strong individuals
   (Tilak, B.C. Pal, Lala Lajpat Rai, Aurobindo Ghosh).
4. **Cultural and intellectual awakening / pride in India's heritage** - the influence of
   Swami Vivekananda, Dayananda Saraswati, and Bankim Chandra Chatterjee (**Anandamath**,
   "**Vande Mataram**"); a reaction against excessive Westernisation.
5. **Reactionary policies of Lord Curzon** - the **Calcutta Corporation Act (1899)**, the
   **Indian Universities Act (1904)**, the **Official Secrets Act (1904)**, and, above all, the
   **Partition of Bengal (1905)** - the immediate trigger.
6. **International influences** - **Ethiopia's (Abyssinia's) defeat of Italy (1896)** and
   **Japan's defeat of Russia (1905)** shattered the myth of European invincibility; plus the
   Boer War and nationalist stirrings in Ireland, Egypt, Turkey and Russia (1905).
7. **An existing militant school of thought** - Rajnarain Bose, Ashwini Kumar Dutt, Vishnu Shastri
   Chiplunkar, and the earlier example of Vasudev Balwant Phadke.

## 2.2 Leaders and ideology
- **Lal-Bal-Pal** - **Lala Lajpat Rai** (Punjab), **Bal Gangadhar Tilak** (Maharashtra),
  **Bipin Chandra Pal** (Bengal) - plus **Aurobindo Ghosh** and **V.O. Chidambaram Pillai**.
- **Goal: Swaraj (self-rule)** - "**Swaraj is my birthright and I shall have it**" (Tilak).
- **Ideology:** hatred of foreign rule; **pride in India's past**; **self-reliance and
  self-determination**; deep **faith in the masses** and in the capacity for self-sacrifice;
  rights to be **demanded as a matter of right**, not begged as favours.

## 2.3 Methods - the four-fold programme
1. **Swadeshi** (promotion of Indian goods and industries).
2. **Boycott** (of British goods, institutions, courts, titles).
3. **National Education** (Indian-run schools and colleges - e.g., the **Bengal National College,
   1906**, with **Aurobindo** as principal).
4. **Passive Resistance** (non-cooperation with, and passive defiance of, the colonial state).
- **Mass mobilisation:** Tilak's **Ganpati Utsav (1893)** and **Shivaji Utsav (1896)**, melas,
  and newspapers (**Kesari** and **Maratha**).

## 2.4 The Swadeshi Movement (1905-1908)
- **Context:** launched in Bengal against the **Partition of Bengal (1905)**.
- **Forms:** boycott and public burning of foreign cloth; setting up **swadeshi enterprises**
  (mills, banks, shops); **national education**; **cultural mobilisation** (Rabindranath Tagore's
  **Rakhi Bandhan** as a symbol of unity, songs, "Vande Mataram"); and some **labour unrest**.
- **Extremist contribution:** they wanted the boycott and swadeshi **spread nationwide**, not
  confined to Bengal, and turned into a movement for **Swaraj**.
- **Limitations:** limited mass/peasant participation, internal splits, and severe repression;
  the movement declined after 1908.

## 2.5 The Surat Split (1907) and repression
- At the **Calcutta session (1906)**, under Extremist pressure, the Congress adopted the four
  resolutions - **Swaraj, Swadeshi, Boycott, and National Education** - and used the word
  **"Swaraj"** as its goal for the first time (Dadabhai Naoroji presided).
- At the **Surat session (1907)**, the Congress **split** into Moderates (led by **Gokhale**) and
  Extremists (led by **Tilak**), over the presidency (Rash Behari Ghosh) and the resolutions; the
  **Extremists walked out**, leaving the Moderates in control.
- **Government repression followed:** **Tilak was jailed for 6 years in Mandalay (Burma), 1908**;
  Aurobindo and B.C. Pal withdrew from active politics; Lajpat Rai left for abroad; the press was
  muzzled by new laws.
- The two wings **reunited at the Lucknow session (1916)**.

## 2.6 Evaluation
- The Extremists were the **first to demand Swaraj as a birthright**, the **first to attempt mass
  mobilisation** on a wide scale, and they gave the movement a **new, confident, self-reliant
  direction**. Their weakness was **weak organisation**, limited durability under repression, and
  the damaging split of 1907.

# 3. MODERATES vs EXTREMISTS (full comparison)

*(Vertical layout - screenshot without side-scrolling.)*

**Time frame**
- Moderates: 1885 to 1905
- Extremists: 1905 to 1919

**Leaders**
- Moderates: Naoroji, Surendranath Banerjee, Pherozeshah Mehta, Gokhale, Wacha
- Extremists: Lal-Bal-Pal, Aurobindo Ghosh, V.O.C. Pillai

**Goal / demand**
- Moderates: gradual constitutional reform; self-government within the Empire
- Extremists: Swaraj (self-rule)

**Methods**
- Moderates: the 3 P's (Petition, Prayer, Protest); constitutional agitation; "political mendicancy"
- Extremists: Swadeshi, Boycott, National Education, Passive Resistance (four-fold programme)

**Attitude to British rule**
- Moderates: faith in British justice; believed in the "providential mission"; apprenticeship needed
- Extremists: no faith; the mission is an illusion; British rule is a design to exploit

**Social and cultural background**
- Moderates: urban educated elite; influenced by the Brahmo Samaj
- Extremists: urban middle class; influenced by the Arya Samaj and cultural revivalism

**Mass base**
- Moderates: narrow (professionals, zamindars); lacked faith in the masses
- Extremists: wider (youth and masses); used festivals to mobilise

**Inspiration**
- Moderates: Western liberal thought (French and American revolutions)
- Extremists: India's own past, culture and traditions

**View on poverty**
- Moderates: caused by India's social and economic structure / British policy
- Extremists: caused by the British presence itself

**Approach to political reform**
- Moderates: persuasion and collaboration
- Extremists: demanded rights as a matter of right

**British response to them**
- Moderates: appeasement / concessions ("carrots")
- Extremists: repression / "sticks"

**Continuity:** both were **stages of one national movement** - the Moderates built the
ideological and organisational base; the Extremists broadened it into an assertive, mass struggle.

# 4. INDIAN COUNCILS ACT, 1861

**Context:** after the Revolt of 1857 and the assumption of Crown rule (Government of India Act
1858), Britain sought to **associate Indians with law-making** and to **reverse the
over-centralisation** created by the Charter Act 1833.

## Key provisions
1. **Portfolio system** given statutory recognition - each Executive Council member was placed in
   charge of a **department** (Military, Revenue, Finance, Home, Law in 1862; **Public Works**
   added in **1874**). The system was **introduced by Lord Canning (1859)**.
2. **Additional (non-official) members** added to the Governor-General's Council for
   **legislative purposes** - **6 to 12**, **nominated**, for a **2-year** term.
3. **Indians associated with law-making for the first time (nominated):** Lord Canning nominated
   the **Raja of Banaras, the Maharaja of Patiala, and Sir Dinkar Rao (1862)**.
4. **Beginning of decentralisation** - **legislative powers restored to the Bombay and Madras
   presidencies**; provision for **new legislative councils** (Bengal 1862, N.W. Provinces 1886,
   Punjab 1897).
5. **Ordinance power** - the Viceroy could issue **ordinances valid for 6 months**.

## Limitations
- The council was **not a real legislature** - it **could not discuss the budget**, **could not
  ask questions**, and had **no control over the executive**; it was purely **advisory** and
  needed the Governor-General's prior sanction for many matters.
- Members were **nominated, not elected**; Indian representation was **token**.

**Significance:** the **beginning of representative institutions** (associating Indians with
legislation) and the **beginning of legislative decentralisation** ("Portfolio + Decentralisation").

# 5. INDIAN COUNCILS ACT, 1892

**Context:** sustained Moderate pressure (petitions, the Bombay Presidency Association, the
British Committee, agitation over the Ilbert Bill and services) pushed Britain to enlarge the
councils.

## Key provisions
1. **Executive Council** - **no change**.
2. **Enlarged legislative councils** - additional (non-official) members in the **Central
   Legislative Council** raised to **not less than 10 and not more than 16** (provincial councils
   also enlarged). As commonly represented: about **6 official + 10 non-official**; of the
   non-officials, **5 nominated by the Viceroy** and **1 each recommended by Bengal, Bombay,
   Madras, the N.W. Provinces, and the Bengal Chamber of Commerce**.
3. **Principle of representation introduced** - the **first (indirect) "election"**: some members
   were nominated **on the recommendation of** provincial councils, municipalities, district
   boards, universities and chambers of commerce. **The word "election" was deliberately avoided**
   - officially it was "**nomination on the recommendation of certain bodies**".
4. **Enlarged functions of members:**
   - **Can discuss the annual budget** - **but cannot vote on it**.
   - **Can put questions** to the executive (with notice) - **but cannot ask supplementary
     questions** and cannot move for adjournment.

## Limitations
- The **official majority continued**; members had **no real power** over the executive or
  finances; the "election" was **indirect and limited**; nationalist aspirations were unmet.

**Significance:** the **first step towards a representative form of government** in India
("Representation + Budget discussion").

## Quick contrast - 1861 vs 1892
- **Budget:** 1861 - cannot discuss | 1892 - can discuss (cannot vote).
- **Questions:** 1861 - cannot ask | 1892 - can ask (no supplementary).
- **Members chosen by:** 1861 - nomination | 1892 - nomination on recommendation (indirect "election").
- **Core idea:** 1861 - Portfolio + Decentralisation | 1892 - Principle of Representation.
