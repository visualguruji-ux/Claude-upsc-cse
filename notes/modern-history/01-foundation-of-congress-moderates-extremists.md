## PART 1 - Regional Integration Theory

From the beginning of the 19th century there were many social-reform organisations that
also worked for political awakening through newspapers, journals and books. These
organisations raised similar issues, which led to the formation of social organisations,
then evolving into regional organisations (such as the Poona Sarvajanik Sabha), and
ultimately culminating into a national-level organisation (the INC).

## PART 2 - Pre-Congress Political Associations

### Early Bengal associations
**Bangabhasha Prakashika Sabha (1836)** - formed by the associates of Raja Rammohan Roy.

**Landholders' Society / Zamindari Association (1838)**
- Founders: Dwarkanath Tagore and Raja Radhakant Deb.
- Worked for the interests of the landlords (zamindars).

**British India Association (1851)**
- Formed by the merger of the Landholders' Society and the Bengal British India Society.
- Associated with Raja Radhakant Deb and Dwarkanath Tagore.

### East India Association (1866)
- Formed by Dadabhai Naoroji in England.
- Aim: to build public opinion in favour of India's political progress.

### Poona Sarvajanik Sabha (1870)
- Founders: G.V. Joshi, S.H. Sathe and S. Chiplunkar (M.G. Ranade joined in 1871, and was
  not a founder).
- Associated later with G.K. Gokhale and Tilak.
- It sent a petition (1875) to the House of Commons demanding the direct representation of
  India in the British Parliament - signed by more than 23,000 people.

### Madras Mahajan Sabha
- Founded by G. Subramania Iyer, P. Anandacharlu and M. Viraraghavachariar.

### Bombay Presidency Association
- Founded by Pherozeshah Mehta, Badruddin Tyabji and K.T. Telang - called the "Bombay Trio".

### Indian National Association (1876)
- Founded by Anand Mohan Bose and Surendranath Banerjee.
- Made demands to Parliament and aimed to grow into a national-level association.

### Mohammedan National Association (1878)
- Founded by Syed Amir Ali.

### Other pre-Congress associations
**British India Society (1839)**
- Founded by William Adam and George Thompson in England.
- Aim: to draw the attention of the British public to despotic rule in India.
- It also published a newspaper called "British Indian Advocate".

**Bengal British India Society (1843)** - formed on the advice of George Thompson.

**Bombay Association (1852)**
- Founded by Jagannath Shankar Seth and Dadabhai Naoroji.
- Aim: to ascertain the demands of the people and present them to the British.

**Deccan Association (1852)**
- Short-lived; intended to send a mission to England to suggest reforms for the Charter Act
  1853, but it failed.

**Madras Native Association (1852)**
- Founded by Gazulu Lakshminarasu Chetty.
- Aim: to raise the issue of peasants under the ryotwari settlement and to criticise the
  reduction in public welfare.

**Indian Parliamentary Committee (1893)**
- Formed by William Wedderburn and W.S. Caine.
- Aim: to gather support for Indian political reform among the British public.

### Demands of the early associations (petition to the British, linked to the Charter Act 1853)
1. Legislation with a popular character.
2. Separation of the executive and the judiciary.
3. Reduction in the salary of officials.
4. Abolition of the salt duty and the stamp duty.

### Assessment of these associations
- They helped in spreading political consciousness, brought the common masses into the
  political fold, and critiqued British law and policy.
- They prepared a strong base for the Congress.
- Limitation: they were limited by a narrow social base, a regional connotation and an
  elite bias - though they imbibed British democratic and constitutional ideas.

## PART 3 - Theories on the Formation of the INC

**Conscious-efforts theory (the most acceptable view)**
- From the above we can deduce that the formation of the INC was not a sudden event. There
  were many conscious efforts by Indians at various levels that ultimately culminated in the
  formation of the INC. This is the most acceptable theory regarding the formation of the
  Congress.
- Historian Bipin Chandra supports this view. He called the initial Congress leaders
  patriotic men of high character who were in no way the stooges of the Government.

**Lightning-Conductor theory - G.K. Gokhale**
- Indian leaders understood that if a platform were established under the leadership of
  Indian nationalists, the British would act to suppress it.
- So they kept A.O. Hume (who was white and an ex-civil servant) in the lead; this shielded
  the Congress from British suppression.
- These early nationalists used the Congress as a lightning conductor - a catalyst to bring
  together the nationalist forces, disguised as a safety wall.

**Conspiracy theory - R.P. Dutt (India Today)**
- It builds on the safety-valve theory.
- According to him, the Congress was the result of a conspiracy to suppress a popular
  uprising in India, and the bourgeois leaders were complacent in it.

## PART 4 - Moderates vs Extremists

*(Formatted vertically so it can be screenshotted without side-scrolling.)*

**Time frame**
- Moderates: 1885 to 1905
- Extremists: 1905 to 1919

**Leaders**
- Moderates: Surendranath Banerjee, Pherozeshah (Firozshah) Mehta, Gokhale
- Extremists: Lal-Bal-Pal, Aurobindo Ghosh, V.O.C. Pillai

**Demand**
- Moderates: gradual constitutional progress; self-rule
- Extremists: Swaraj

**Method**
- Moderates: prayer, petition, protest (the 3 P's); constitutional agitation; philanthropy.
  Their approach was criticised as "political mendicancy" (politics of begging).
- Extremists: passive resistance

**Attitude towards the British**
- Moderates: believed in the providential mission of British rule; had faith in the British
  sense of justice; felt British apprenticeship was necessary for political progress.
- Extremists: the providential mission is propaganda and an illusion; no faith in it; it is
  just a design to further British rule in India.

**Social and cultural background**
- Moderates: urban elite; Brahmo Samaj
- Extremists: urban middle class; Arya Samaj

**Mass base**
- Moderates: narrow (landlords); lacked faith in the masses
- Extremists: wider (reached the youth and the masses); Tilak used the Ganesha festival and
  the Shivaji festival for mass mobilisation

**Inspiration**
- Moderates: Western liberal thought (the French Revolution, the American Revolution)
- Extremists: India's past tradition and institutions

**On poverty**
- Moderates: caused by the social and economic structure in the country
- Extremists: caused by the British presence

**On political reform**
- Moderates: persuasion / collaborative
- Extremists: demanded it as a matter of right

**British attitude towards them**
- Moderates: appeasement / promotion (carrots)
- Extremists: isolation / negation (sticks)

### Moderates and Extremists - the interaction
- The Moderates were initially satisfied with reform, but under the pressure of the
  Extremists they raised the slogan "No taxation without representation".
- Bal Gangadhar Tilak commented: "Political rights will have to be fought for. The Moderates
  think they can be won by persuasion; we think they can be obtained only by strong pressure."

## PART 5 - Indian Councils Act, 1861

**Portfolio system**
- Introduced by Canning in 1858; the 1861 Act gave it recognition.
- Departments (1862): Military, Revenue, Finance, Home, Law. Public Works was added in 1874.

**Ordinance** - the Viceroy could issue an ordinance valid for 6 months.

**Central legislative body** - the earlier over-centralisation was reversed; this Act marks
the beginning of decentralisation in India.

**Additional members (6 to 10)**
- Cannot ask questions to the Executive Council.
- Cannot discuss the Budget.
- Term of 2 years.
- Two categories:
  - Official.
  - Non-official: Sir Dinkar Rao, the Raja of Banaras, the Maharaja of Patiala.

**Other points**
- Gradually legislatures were set up in the N.W. Provinces, Punjab and Burma as well.
- The Act gave the Viceroy the liberty to increase the members.
- The Governor-General's Council was expanded for the purpose of making law.

## PART 6 - Indian Councils Act, 1892

**Executive Council** - no change.

**Additional Members (10 to 16)**
- Official: 6.
- Non-official: 10, of which:
  - 5 were appointed by the Viceroy.
  - 1 each from Bengal, Bombay, Madras, the N.W. Provinces, and the Bengal Chamber of Commerce.

**Powers of the members**
- Can ask questions to the Executive Council.
- Can discuss financial matters (the Budget).
- Cannot vote on the Budget.
- Cannot ask supplementary questions.

**Significance**
- This Act introduced the principle of representation.
- It was the first step towards a representative form of government.
- For the first time there was indirect election - though they called it not "election" but
  "nomination on the basis of recommendation by certain bodies".
