# PART I - Growth of Political Consciousness (the "Regional Integration" idea)

From the early 19th century, an English-educated Indian middle class emerged (lawyers,
teachers, journalists, doctors). Through newspapers, journals and books, social-reform
and literary bodies also began political awakening.

- These bodies raised similar grievances, so scattered **local associations** slowly
  matured into **regional associations**, and finally these regional streams
  **integrated into one national organisation - the Indian National Congress (1885)**.
- This is the **"Regional Integration"** understanding: the Congress was the culmination
  and integration of pre-existing regional political activity, not a sudden creation.

**Exam pointer (Mains):** Use this as the opening thesis in any "foundation of Congress"
answer - the INC was "not a sudden mushroom growth" (Bipan Chandra) but the organised
expression of a maturing national consciousness.

# PART II - Political Associations Before the Congress

These are best remembered in **two phases**.

## Phase 1 (1830s - 1850s): Early associations, dominated by landed/aristocratic elites

| Year | Association | City | Founder / Key figures | Points to remember |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1836 | **Bangabhasha Prakashika Sabha** | Calcutta | Associates of **Raja Rammohan Roy** | Among the earliest political associations |
| 1838 | **Landholders' Society** (Zamindari Association) | Calcutta | **Dwarkanath Tagore**, Raja Radhakant Deb | Regarded as the **first organised political effort**; used constitutional agitation; safeguarded **zamindar/landlord** interests |
| 1843 | **Bengal British India Society** | Calcutta | Formed on the advice of **George Thompson** | Aim: collect and publish information on the real condition of the people |
| 1851 | **British Indian Association** | Calcutta | Merger of the Landholders' Society + Bengal British India Society (President: **Raja Radhakant Deb**; Secretary: **Debendranath Tagore**) | 1852 petition to Parliament sought a separate legislature, separation of executive and judiciary, abolition of certain taxes (salt, abkari, stamp), and reduction of official salaries |
| 1852 | **Bombay Association** | Bombay | **Jagannath Shankar Seth**, Dadabhai Naoroji | First political association of the Bombay Presidency; sent petitions on the **Charter Act 1853** |
| 1852 | **Madras Native Association** | Madras | **Gazulu Lakshminarasu Chetty** | Affiliate-style body; raised **ryotwari peasant** grievances; short-lived |
| 1852 | **Deccan Association** | Deccan/Poona region | (local reformers) | **Short-lived**; intended to send a memorandum/mission to England for reforms under the Charter Act 1853, but the effort largely failed |

> Do not confuse the three "British India" bodies:
> - **British India Society** = **1839, London**, by **William Adam** (with George Thompson) - to draw the attention of the British public and Parliament to conditions in India.
> - **Bengal British India Society** = **1843, Calcutta**.
> - **British Indian Association** = **1851, Calcutta** (the merger of Landholders' Society + Bengal British India Society).

**Character of Phase 1:** dominated by wealthy and aristocratic elements (zamindars,
big merchants); **provincial/local** in character; loyalist; worked to safeguard
class interests through petitions.

## Phase 2 (1860s - 1880s): More nationalist, broader-based associations

| Year | Association | Founder / Key figures | Points to remember |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1865 | **London Indian Society** | **Dadabhai Naoroji**, W.C. Bonnerjee | Formed in London to discuss Indian political questions; soon merged into the East India Association |
| 1866 | **East India Association** | **Dadabhai Naoroji** (London) | To discuss Indian questions and **influence British public opinion / Members of Parliament** in favour of India; later had branches in India |
| 1870 | **Poona Sarvajanik Sabha** | **M.G. Ranade** (with G.V. Joshi, S.H. Sathe, S.H. Chiplunkar) | A bridge between the government and the people; **1875 petition** to the British **House of Commons** demanding India's **direct representation** in the British Parliament, **signed by over 23,000 people**; nurtured **Gokhale** and **Tilak** |
| 1876 | **Indian Association** (Indian National Association) | **Surendranath Banerjee** and **Anand Mohan Bose** (Calcutta) | The **first avowedly nationalist** organisation; aimed to create a strong all-India public opinion and to be an **all-India centre**; led the **Ilbert Bill** agitation and the anti-**Civil Service age-limit** agitation; **merged into the INC in 1886** |
| 1878 | **National Mohammedan Association** (Central National Muhammadan Association) | **Syed Amir Ali** | Political platform to voice Muslim concerns |
| 1884 | **Madras Mahajan Sabha** | **M. Viraraghavachariar, G. Subramania Iyer, P. Anandacharlu** | A regional forerunner of the Congress in the South |
| 1885 | **Bombay Presidency Association** | **Pherozeshah Mehta, K.T. Telang, Badruddin Tyabji** - the **"Bombay Trio"** | Demanded a larger Indian role in the legislative councils and services; part of the ground that led to the **Indian Councils Act 1892** |
| 1893 | **Indian Parliamentary Committee** | **William Wedderburn** and **W.S. Caine** | Formed in Britain to gather support among the British public and MPs for Indian political reform |

**Memory hook:** Bombay Trio = **M-T-T** (Mehta, Telang, Tyabji).

## Common demands of the early associations (fed the Charter Act 1853 petitions)
1. A **legislature with a popular / representative character**.
2. **Separation of the executive from the judiciary**.
3. **Indianisation** of services and **reduction in the salaries** of high (European) officials.
4. **Abolition of oppressive taxes** - salt duty, stamp duty, abkari (excise).
5. Spread of **education** (including establishment of universities).

## Assessment of the pre-Congress associations (Mains value-addition)
**Contribution:** spread political consciousness; trained the educated classes in political
work; developed the technique of constitutional agitation; critiqued British law and policy;
**prepared the base for a single national organisation**.

**Limitations:** **narrow social base**; **regional / parochial** outlook (each confined to a
presidency); **elite bias** (zamindars, professionals); worked **within the colonial system**,
having imbibed British democratic and constitutional ideas. These limits are precisely what
the all-India Congress (1885) was created to overcome.

# PART III - Foundation of the Indian National Congress (1885)

- Founded in **December 1885** at **Bombay** (Gokuldas Tejpal Sanskrit College).
- **First session:** 28-31 December 1885; **72 delegates**; President **W.C. Bonnerjee**.
- **A.O. Hume** (a retired British ICS officer) was the **general secretary / prime mover** in
  organising it. (Originally the "Indian National Union"; renamed Indian National Congress.)

## Theories of the formation of the Congress

**1. Safety-Valve Theory.**
The Congress was allegedly created as a **"safety valve"** to release growing popular discontent
and prevent a violent revolt (a "1857 x 10"). Advocates cite that **A.O. Hume** possessed
**"seven volumes" of secret reports** at Simla warning of imminent unrest, and that Hume acted
with the knowledge of Viceroy **Lord Dufferin** to channel discontent into a safe,
constitutional outlet. (This early idea appears in **William Wedderburn's** biography of Hume.)

**2. Conspiracy / "Safety-valve as a plot" Theory - R.P. Dutt.**
In **India Today (1940)**, the Marxist historian **R.P. Dutt** argued the Congress was born out
of a **conspiracy** to abort a popular (peasant/mass) uprising; the emerging **bourgeois**
leadership was a complacent partner. (An extension of the safety-valve idea.)

**3. The extremists' use of the theory - Lala Lajpat Rai.**
Radical leaders (**Lala Lajpat Rai** in *Young India / Unhappy India*, and Tilak, B.C. Pal)
turned the safety-valve claim against the moderates - arguing the Congress had been a device to
protect British rule, and that Indians must now fight for rights.

**4. Lightning-Conductor Theory - G.K. Gokhale.**
The moderate leaders **deliberately** kept **A.O. Hume** and other retired Englishmen in the
lead as a **shield / lightning conductor**: had Indians openly founded it, the suspicious
Government would have crushed the infant body. Hume was used to protect and channel the
nationalist forces safely. (Gokhale is quoted to this effect.)

**5. Conscious-Efforts view - the accepted position (Bipan Chandra).**
Modern historians, led by **Bipan Chandra**, have **debunked the "seven volumes" and the
safety-valve thesis as a myth** (the volumes, on investigation, were about religious/social
matters, not political revolt). The Congress was the **culmination of decades of conscious,
sustained political effort** by Indians; Hume's role was that of a facilitator ("lightning
conductor"), not creator. The early leaders were **patriots of high character**, not
government "stooges" (a view Bipan Chandra defends strongly).

**Exam pointer:** For a Mains answer, structure it as - the safety-valve claim (and its
seven-volumes "proof") -> its extension by R.P. Dutt (conspiracy) and use by Lajpat Rai ->
the counter (lightning conductor) -> conclude with Bipan Chandra's accepted view that the
Congress was the product of India's own political awakening.

# PART IV - The Moderates (Early Nationalists), 1885 - 1905

## Who they were
Leaders included **Dadabhai Naoroji, Dinshaw Wacha, Pherozeshah Mehta, Surendranath Banerjee,
Gopal Krishna Gokhale, Badruddin Tyabji, M.G. Ranade, W.C. Bonnerjee, Madan Mohan Malaviya,
G. Subramania Iyer**. Mostly English-educated professionals influenced by Western liberal and
democratic thought.

## Ideology / beliefs
- **Faith in the British sense of justice** and in the "providential mission" of British rule
  (believing British rule could be reformed from within).
- Believed a period of **political apprenticeship** was necessary before self-government.
- Loyal to the Crown; wanted reform, **not** the end of British rule.

## Methods
- **Constitutional agitation** strictly "within the four corners of the law."
- Popularly summed up as the **"3 P's": Petition, Prayer, Protest** - i.e., petitions,
  memorials, resolutions, meetings, deputations, and articles in the press, aimed at
  influencing the British Government, Parliament and public opinion.
  - **Note on "3 P's vs 4 P's":** the textbook standard is **3 P's**. Some coaching materials
    add a **4th P - "Propaganda"** (a.k.a. political education / **Persuasion**), for the
    moderates' sustained work of **educating public opinion in India and Britain**. So if an
    examiner expects four, it is **Petition, Prayer, Protest, and Propaganda**.
- **Work in Britain:** set up the **British Committee of the Indian National Congress (1889)**
  in London, which ran a journal called **"India"**; **Dadabhai Naoroji** spent years in Britain
  and even became a **British MP (1892, Liberal, Central Finsbury)** to plead India's case.
- "The Method of the Moderates was **prayer and petition**, and if that failed, protest -
  all within constitutional limits."

## Demands
**Constitutional / political:** expansion and reform of legislative councils; more Indians in
the councils; **greater powers to councils** (over the budget, right to question).

**Administrative:** **Indianisation of the civil services**; **holding the ICS examination
simultaneously in India and England** and raising the age limit; separation of the judiciary
from the executive; repeal of the **Arms Act** and repressive laws; more welfare spending.

**Economic (the biggest contribution):** a powerful **economic critique of colonialism** -
- **Dadabhai Naoroji's "Drain of Wealth" theory** (in **Poverty and Un-British Rule in India**,
  1901): Britain was draining India's wealth, causing poverty.
- **R.C. Dutt's** *Economic History of India*.
- Demands to reduce **land revenue** and **military/"Home Charges"** expenditure, develop modern
  industry, and protect Indian handicrafts.

## Achievements of the Moderates
- **Indian Councils Act 1892** (enlarged councils, budget discussion, questions).
- Appointment of the **Public Service Commission (1886)** (Aitchison Commission).
- A **House of Commons resolution (1893)** for **simultaneous ICS examinations** in India and Britain.
- The **Welby Commission (Royal Commission on Indian Expenditure, 1895)** with **Dadabhai Naoroji**
  as a member.
- Most importantly, they **exposed the true economic character of British rule** and created a
  **national political consciousness** and a common all-India platform.

## Evaluation / criticism
- Their **social base was narrow** (urban educated elite); they "**lacked faith in the masses**"
  and did not mobilise them.
- **Slow, limited results** led younger nationalists to deride their methods as
  **"political mendicancy"** (politics of begging). Yet they laid the essential foundations -
  organisation, ideology, and an economic critique - on which later mass movements were built.

# PART V - The Extremists (Assertive Nationalists), 1905 - 1919

## Causes of the rise of Extremism
1. **Realisation of the true nature of British rule** - the economic drain, recurring **famines
   (1896-97, 1899-1900)** and the **plague**, exposed the "un-British" reality.
2. **Failure/slow pace of moderate methods** - petitions yielded little; a new generation
   grew impatient.
3. **Growth of self-respect and confidence** and a **militant school of thought** (Tilak,
   Bipin Chandra Pal, Lala Lajpat Rai, Aurobindo Ghosh) drawing pride from India's past.
4. **Reactionary policies of Lord Curzon** - the **Calcutta Corporation Act (1899)**, the
   **Indian Universities Act (1904)**, the **Official Secrets Act**, and above all the
   **Partition of Bengal (1905)**, the immediate trigger.
5. **International influences** - **Italy's defeat by Ethiopia (Abyssinia, 1896)** and
   **Japan's defeat of Russia (1905)** shattered the myth of European/white invincibility;
   also the Boer Wars, and nationalist movements in Ireland, Egypt, etc.

## Leaders and ideology
- **Lal-Bal-Pal** = **Lala Lajpat Rai** (Punjab), **Bal Gangadhar Tilak** (Maharashtra),
  **Bipin Chandra Pal** (Bengal); plus **Aurobindo Ghosh** and **V.O. Chidambaram Pillai**.
- **Goal: Swaraj (self-rule).** Tilak: **"Swaraj is my birthright and I shall have it."**
- Drew inspiration from **India's own past, culture and traditions**; wanted rights claimed
  **as a matter of right**, not begged as favours.

## Methods - Tilak's four-point programme
1. **Swadeshi** (use of Indian-made goods; boosting indigenous industry).
2. **Boycott** (of British goods and institutions).
3. **National Education** (Indian-run schools and colleges).
4. **Passive Resistance** (non-cooperation with the colonial administration).
- **Mass mobilisation** using **festivals** - Tilak's **Ganpati Utsav (1893)** and
  **Shivaji Utsav (1896)** - to spread nationalist feeling among the people.

## The Surat Split (1907)
The Moderate-Extremist tension culminated in the **split of the Congress at the Surat session
(1907)**. The party reunited only later (Lucknow session, 1916). Under the pressure of the
Extremists, even the Moderates sharpened their tone and adopted the slogan
**"No taxation without representation."**

> **Tilak's line (from the notes):** "Political rights will have to be fought for. The Moderates
> think they can be won by persuasion; we think they can be obtained only by strong pressure."

# PART VI - Moderates vs Extremists (comparison)

| Basis | Moderates (1885 - 1905) | Extremists (1905 - 1919) |
|---|---|---|
| **Leaders** | Naoroji, S.N. Banerjee, Pherozeshah Mehta, Gokhale, Wacha | Lal-Bal-Pal, Aurobindo Ghosh, V.O.C. Pillai |
| **Goal** | Gradual reform; self-government **within** the Empire | **Swaraj** (self-rule) |
| **Methods** | Constitutional agitation; **3 P's** (Petition, Prayer, Protest) [+ Propaganda] | Swadeshi, boycott, national education, passive resistance; mass agitation |
| **Attitude to British rule** | Faith in British justice; believed in the "providential mission"; apprenticeship needed | No faith; the "mission" is an illusion; rule is a design to exploit |
| **Inspiration** | Western liberal thought (French and American revolutions) | India's own past, culture and traditions |
| **Social base** | Urban educated **elite**; narrow; "lacked faith in the masses" | Urban middle class; reached out to **youth and wider masses** |
| **Reform influence** | Brahmo Samaj | Arya Samaj |
| **View on poverty** | Caused by India's social and economic structure / British policy | Caused by the **British presence** itself |
| **Approach to reform** | Persuasion / collaboration | Rights demanded **as a matter of right** |
| **British response** | Appeasement / "carrots" | Repression / "sticks" |

**Continuity, not just contrast:** both were stages of one national movement - the Moderates
built the ideological and organisational base; the Extremists broadened it into an assertive,
mass-oriented struggle.

# PART VII - Indian Councils Act, 1861

**Context:** After the Revolt of 1857 and the Government of India Act 1858 (Crown rule), Britain
sought to associate Indians with law-making and to reverse the over-centralisation of the
**Charter Act 1833**.

## Key provisions
1. **Portfolio system** given statutory recognition - each member of the Executive Council was
   put in charge of a **department** (Military, Revenue, Finance, Home, Law - 1862;
   **Public Works** added in **1874**). The system was **introduced by Lord Canning (1859)** and
   **recognised by the 1861 Act**.
2. **Additional (non-official) members** added to the Governor-General's Council for **legislative
   purposes** - **6 to 12 members**, **nominated**, for a **2-year** term.
3. **Indians associated with law-making for the first time (nominated).** Lord Canning nominated
   the **Raja of Banaras, the Maharaja of Patiala, and Sir Dinkar Rao (1862)**.
4. **Beginning of decentralisation** - **legislative powers restored to the Bombay and Madras**
   presidencies; and provision to create **new legislative councils** - **Bengal (1862),
   North-Western Provinces (1886), Punjab (1897)**.
5. **Ordinance power** - the Viceroy could issue **ordinances (valid 6 months)** in an emergency
   without the council.
6. The Viceroy could make **rules for the conduct of business** and could **veto** council acts.

## Limitations
- The legislative council was **not a real legislature** - it could not discuss the **budget**,
  could not **ask questions**, and had **no control over the executive**; it was **purely
  advisory**, and needed the Governor-General's prior sanction for many matters.
- Members were **nominated, not elected**; Indian representation was **token/symbolic**.

**Significance / keyword:** **"Portfolio + Decentralisation."** It marks the **beginning of
representative institutions** (associating Indians with legislation) and the **beginning of
legislative devolution** to the provinces.

# PART VIII - Indian Councils Act, 1892

**Context:** Sustained moderate pressure (petitions, the Bombay Presidency Association, the
British Committee) pushed Britain to enlarge the councils.

## Key provisions
1. **Executive Council** - **no change**.
2. **Enlarged legislative councils** - the number of **additional (non-official) members** in the
   **Central Legislative Council** was raised to **not less than 10 and not more than 16**
   (provincial councils were similarly enlarged).
   - Composition (as commonly represented): about **6 official + 10 non-official**; of the
     non-officials, roughly **5 nominated by the Viceroy** and **1 each recommended by Bengal,
     Bombay, Madras, the North-Western Provinces, and the Bengal Chamber of Commerce**.
3. **Principle of representation introduced** - the **first (indirect) "election"**: some
   non-official members were nominated by the Governor-General/Governors **on the recommendation
   of** provincial legislative councils, municipalities, district boards, universities and
   chambers of commerce. **The word "election" was deliberately avoided** - officially it was
   "**nomination on the recommendation of certain bodies**."
4. **Enlarged functions of members:**
   - **Can discuss the annual budget** (financial statement) - **but cannot vote on it**.
   - **Can put questions** to the executive (with prior notice) - **but cannot ask supplementary
     questions** and cannot move for adjournment.

## Limitations
- The **official majority continued**; members had **no real power** over the executive or
  finances; the "election" was **indirect and limited**; nationalist aspirations were unmet.

**Significance / keyword:** **"Representation + Budget discussion."** It is called the
**first step towards a representative form of government** in India.

## Quick contrast - 1861 vs 1892 (a favourite Prelims trap)

| Feature | 1861 | 1892 |
|---|---|---|
| Budget | Cannot discuss | **Can discuss** (cannot vote) |
| Questions | Cannot ask | **Can ask** (no supplementary) |
| How members chosen | Nomination | **Nomination on recommendation** (indirect "election") |
| Core idea | Portfolio + Decentralisation | Principle of Representation |
