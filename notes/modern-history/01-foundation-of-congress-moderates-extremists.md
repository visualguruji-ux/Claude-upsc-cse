## PART 1 - Growth of Political Consciousness (Regional Integration)

From the early 19th century an English-educated Indian middle class (lawyers, teachers,
journalists) emerged. Social-reform and literary bodies did political awakening through
newspapers, journals and books. They raised similar grievances, so scattered local
associations gradually matured into regional associations, and these regional streams
finally integrated into one national organisation - the Indian National Congress (1885).

This is the "Regional Integration" idea: the Congress was the culmination and integration
of pre-existing regional political activity, not a sudden creation.

## PART 2 - Pre-Congress Political Associations

### Phase 1 (1830s-1850s): aristocratic / landed elites, provincial in character

**Bangabhasha Prakashika Sabha (1836)** - formed by the associates of Raja Rammohan Roy;
among the earliest political associations.

**Landholders' Society / Zamindari Association (1838)**
- Founders: Dwarkanath Tagore and Raja Radhakant Deb.
- The first organised political effort; used constitutional agitation to guard zamindar (landlord) interests.

**Bengal British India Society (1843)** - formed on the advice of George Thompson; aim: to
collect and publish information on the real condition of the people.

**British India Association (1851)**
- Formed by the merger of the Landholders' Society and the Bengal British India Society
  (President: Raja Radhakant Deb; Secretary: Debendranath Tagore).
- Its 1852 petition to Parliament sought a separate legislature, separation of the executive
  and judiciary, and abolition of certain taxes.

**Bombay Association (1852)** - founders Jagannath Shankar Seth and Dadabhai Naoroji; aim: to
ascertain the demands of the people and present them to the British.

**Madras Native Association (1852)** - founded by Gazulu Lakshminarasu Chetty; raised the
grievances of peasants under the ryotwari settlement and criticised the reduction in public
welfare; short-lived.

**Deccan Association (1852)** - short-lived; tried to send a mission to England to suggest
reforms for the Charter Act 1853, but failed.

> Do not confuse the three "British India" bodies:
> - British India Society = 1839, London, by William Adam (with George Thompson) - to draw the British public's attention to despotic rule in India; it also published a newspaper, "British Indian Advocate".
> - Bengal British India Society = 1843, Calcutta.
> - British Indian Association = 1851, Calcutta (the merger).

### Phase 2 (1860s-1880s): more nationalist, broader-based

**London Indian Society (1865)** - formed by Dadabhai Naoroji and W.C. Bonnerjee in London to
discuss Indian political questions; soon merged into the East India Association.

**East India Association (1866)** - formed by Dadabhai Naoroji in London; aim: to build public
opinion (and influence Members of Parliament) in favour of India's political progress; later
had branches in India.

**Poona Sarvajanik Sabha (1870)**
- Founders: G.V. Joshi, S.H. Sathe, S. Chiplunkar (M.G. Ranade joined in 1871 and was not a founder; the Sabha later nurtured Gokhale and Tilak).
- Its 1875 petition to the House of Commons demanded India's direct representation in the British Parliament - signed by more than 23,000 people.

**Indian National Association (1876)** - founded by Surendranath Banerjee and Anand Mohan Bose
in Calcutta; the first avowedly nationalist body; led the Ilbert Bill and Civil Service
age-limit agitations; aimed to be an all-India centre; merged into the INC in 1886.

**Mohammedan National Association (1878)** - founded by Syed Amir Ali; a political platform to
voice Muslim concerns.

**Madras Mahajan Sabha (1884)** - founders G. Subramania Iyer, P. Anandacharlu and
M. Viraraghavachariar; a regional forerunner of the Congress in the South.

**Bombay Presidency Association (1885)** - founders Pherozeshah Mehta, Badruddin Tyabji and
K.T. Telang (the "Bombay Trio"); demanded a larger Indian role in the councils and services.

**Indian Parliamentary Committee (1893)** - formed by William Wedderburn and W.S. Caine in
Britain to gather support for Indian political reform among the British public.

### Demands of the early associations (linked to the Charter Act 1853)
1. A legislature with a popular / representative character.
2. Separation of the executive from the judiciary.
3. Indianisation of services and reduction in the salaries of high officials.
4. Abolition of oppressive taxes - the salt duty and the stamp duty.

### Assessment
- They spread political consciousness, brought the masses into the political fold, trained
  the educated classes in political work, critiqued British law and policy, and prepared a
  strong base for the Congress.
- Limitations: a narrow social base, a regional / parochial outlook and an elite bias -
  though they imbibed British democratic and constitutional ideas.

## PART 3 - Foundation of the INC (1885) and the Theories of its Formation

The Congress was founded in December 1885 at Bombay; A.O. Hume (a retired British ICS officer)
was the prime mover, and W.C. Bonnerjee was the first president (72 delegates).

**Safety-valve theory.** The Congress was allegedly created as a "safety valve" to release
growing popular discontent and prevent a violent revolt bigger than 1857. Advocates cite that
Hume possessed "seven volumes" of secret reports at Simla warning of unrest, and that he acted
with the knowledge of Viceroy Lord Dufferin to channel discontent into a safe, constitutional
outlet.

**Conspiracy theory - R.P. Dutt (India Today, 1940).** Building on the safety-valve idea, the
Congress was the result of a conspiracy to abort a popular (mass) uprising, and the emerging
bourgeois leadership was a complacent partner.

**The extremists' use of the theory - Lala Lajpat Rai.** Radical leaders turned the
safety-valve claim against the Moderates, arguing the Congress had been a device to protect
British rule and that Indians must now fight for their rights.

**Lightning-conductor theory - G.K. Gokhale.** The Moderate leaders deliberately kept A.O. Hume
(white, an ex-civil servant) in the lead as a shield: had Indians openly founded it, the
suspicious Government would have suppressed the infant body. Hume was used as a lightning
conductor to protect and channel the nationalist forces.

**Conscious-efforts view - the accepted position (Bipin Chandra).** Modern historians, led by
Bipin Chandra, have debunked the "seven volumes" and the safety-valve thesis as a myth. The
Congress was the culmination of decades of conscious, sustained Indian effort; Hume's role was
that of a facilitator, not a creator; and the early leaders were patriots of high character,
not government stooges.

## PART 4 - The Moderates (Early Nationalists), 1885-1905

**Who:** Dadabhai Naoroji, Dinshaw Wacha, Pherozeshah Mehta, Surendranath Banerjee,
G.K. Gokhale, Badruddin Tyabji, M.G. Ranade, W.C. Bonnerjee - English-educated professionals.

**Ideology:** faith in the British sense of justice and in the "providential mission" of
British rule (believing it could be reformed from within); a period of political apprenticeship
was thought necessary; loyal to the Crown.

**Methods:**
- Constitutional agitation "within the four corners of the law".
- The "3 P's": Petition, Prayer, Protest - petitions, memorials, resolutions, meetings,
  deputations, press articles. (Some coaching materials add a 4th P, "Propaganda" / political
  education.)
- Work in Britain: the British Committee of the INC (1889) and its journal "India"; Dadabhai
  Naoroji even became a British MP (1892) to plead India's case.
- Their approach was later derided by younger nationalists as "political mendicancy" (politics
  of begging).

**Demands:**
- Constitutional: expansion and reform of the legislative councils; more Indians in them;
  greater powers over the budget and the right to question.
- Administrative: Indianisation of the civil services; holding the ICS examination
  simultaneously in India and England and raising the age limit; separation of the judiciary
  from the executive; repeal of the Arms Act.
- Economic (their biggest contribution): the "Drain of Wealth" critique - Dadabhai Naoroji
  (Poverty and Un-British Rule in India, 1901) and R.C. Dutt (Economic History of India);
  demands to cut land revenue and military / "Home Charges" expenditure and to develop industry.

**Achievements:** the Indian Councils Act 1892; the Public Service Commission (1886); a House
of Commons resolution (1893) for simultaneous ICS examinations; the Welby Commission on Indian
expenditure (1895, with Naoroji as a member); and, above all, exposing the economic character
of British rule and creating an all-India political consciousness.

**Evaluation:** a narrow social base and limited mass contact ("lacked faith in the masses"),
and slow results - yet they laid the organisation, ideology and economic critique on which
later mass movements were built.

## PART 5 - The Extremists (Assertive Nationalists), 1905-1919

**Causes of the rise of Extremism:**
1. Recognition of the true (exploitative) nature of British rule - the economic drain, famines
   (1896-97, 1899-1900) and plague.
2. The slow, meagre results of Moderate methods.
3. Growth of self-respect and confidence, and a militant school of thought drawing pride from
   India's past (Tilak, B.C. Pal, Lala Lajpat Rai, Aurobindo Ghosh).
4. The reactionary policies of Lord Curzon - the Calcutta Corporation Act (1899), the Indian
   Universities Act (1904), the Official Secrets Act, and, above all, the Partition of Bengal
   (1905) - the immediate trigger.
5. International influences - Ethiopia's defeat of Italy (1896) and Japan's defeat of Russia
   (1905) shattered the myth of European invincibility.

**Leaders and ideology:** Lal-Bal-Pal (Lala Lajpat Rai, Bal Gangadhar Tilak, Bipin Chandra
Pal), plus Aurobindo Ghosh and V.O. Chidambaram Pillai. Goal: Swaraj - "Swaraj is my birthright
and I shall have it" (Tilak). They drew on India's own past and claimed rights as a matter of
right, not as favours.

**Methods (Tilak's four-point programme):** Swadeshi, Boycott, National Education, and Passive
Resistance; and mass mobilisation through festivals - Tilak's Ganpati Utsav (1893) and Shivaji
Utsav (1896).

**The Surat Split (1907):** Moderate-Extremist tension split the Congress at Surat; it reunited
at the Lucknow session (1916). Under Extremist pressure the Moderates too sharpened their tone,
raising the slogan "No taxation without representation".

> Tilak: "Political rights will have to be fought for. The Moderates think they can be won by
> persuasion; we think they can be obtained only by strong pressure."

## PART 6 - Moderates vs Extremists (full comparison)

*(Vertical layout - screenshot without side-scrolling.)*

**Time frame**
- Moderates: 1885 to 1905
- Extremists: 1905 to 1919

**Leaders**
- Moderates: Naoroji, Surendranath Banerjee, Pherozeshah Mehta, Gokhale
- Extremists: Lal-Bal-Pal, Aurobindo Ghosh, V.O.C. Pillai

**Demand**
- Moderates: gradual constitutional progress; self-government within the Empire
- Extremists: Swaraj

**Method**
- Moderates: prayer, petition, protest (the 3 P's); constitutional agitation; philanthropy - criticised as "political mendicancy"
- Extremists: passive resistance; swadeshi, boycott and national education

**Attitude towards the British**
- Moderates: believed in the providential mission; faith in the British sense of justice; apprenticeship necessary for progress
- Extremists: the mission is propaganda and an illusion; no faith; it is a design to further British rule

**Social and cultural background**
- Moderates: urban elite; Brahmo Samaj
- Extremists: urban middle class; Arya Samaj

**Mass base**
- Moderates: narrow (landlords); lacked faith in the masses
- Extremists: wider (youth and masses); Tilak used the Ganesha and Shivaji festivals to mobilise

**Inspiration**
- Moderates: Western liberal thought (the French and American revolutions)
- Extremists: India's past tradition and institutions

**On poverty**
- Moderates: caused by the social and economic structure of the country
- Extremists: caused by the British presence

**On political reform**
- Moderates: persuasion / collaborative
- Extremists: demanded it as a matter of right

**British attitude towards them**
- Moderates: appeasement / promotion (carrots)
- Extremists: isolation / negation (sticks)

**Continuity:** both were stages of one movement - the Moderates built the ideological and
organisational base; the Extremists broadened it into an assertive, mass-oriented struggle.

## PART 7 - Indian Councils Act, 1861

**Context:** after the Revolt of 1857 and Crown rule (1858), Britain sought to associate Indians
with law-making and to reverse the over-centralisation of the Charter Act 1833.

- **Portfolio system:** introduced by Canning (1858-59), recognised by this Act. Departments
  (1862): Military, Revenue, Finance, Home, Law; Public Works added in 1874.
- **Additional (non-official) members** for legislation: 6 to 12, nominated, 2-year term.
- **Indians associated with law-making for the first time (nominated):** the Raja of Banaras,
  the Maharaja of Patiala and Sir Dinkar Rao (nominated by Canning, 1862).
- **Beginning of decentralisation:** legislative powers restored to the Bombay and Madras
  presidencies; new legislative councils later for Bengal (1862), the N.W. Provinces (1886) and
  Punjab (1897).
- **Ordinance power:** the Viceroy could issue ordinances valid for 6 months.
- **Limitations:** the council was purely advisory - it could not discuss the budget, could not
  ask questions, and had no control over the executive; members were nominated, not elected.

**Significance:** the beginning of representative institutions (associating Indians with
legislation) and the beginning of legislative decentralisation.

## PART 8 - Indian Councils Act, 1892

**Context:** sustained Moderate pressure (petitions, the Bombay Presidency Association, the
British Committee) pushed Britain to enlarge the councils.

- **Executive Council:** no change.
- **Additional (non-official) members** in the Central Legislative Council raised to 10-16
  (provincial councils also enlarged). As commonly represented: about 6 official + 10
  non-official - of the non-officials, 5 nominated by the Viceroy and 1 each from Bengal,
  Bombay, Madras, the N.W. Provinces and the Bengal Chamber of Commerce.
- **Principle of representation introduced** - the first (indirect) "election": some members
  were nominated on the recommendation of provincial councils, municipalities, universities and
  chambers of commerce. The word "election" was deliberately avoided - officially it was
  "nomination on the recommendation of certain bodies".
- **Enlarged functions:** members could discuss the budget (but not vote on it) and could ask
  questions (but not supplementary questions).
- **Limitations:** the official majority continued; no real power over the executive or
  finances; the "election" was indirect and limited.

**Significance:** the first step towards a representative form of government in India.

**Quick contrast (1861 vs 1892):**
- Budget: 1861 - cannot discuss; 1892 - can discuss (cannot vote).
- Questions: 1861 - cannot ask; 1892 - can ask (no supplementary).
- Members: 1861 - nomination; 1892 - nomination on recommendation (indirect "election").
- Core idea: 1861 - Portfolio + Decentralisation; 1892 - Principle of Representation.
