Foundation of the INC, Moderates and Extremists, and the Councils Acts

Part 1

Growth of Political Consciousness (Regional Integration)

From the early 19th century an English-educated Indian middle class emerged. Social-reform and literary bodies did political awakening through newspapers, journals and books. They raised similar grievances, so scattered local associations matured into regional ones, and these regional streams finally integrated into one national organisation - the INC (1885). The Congress was the culmination of pre-existing regional political activity, not a sudden creation.

Part 2

Pre-Congress Political Associations

Phase 1 (1830s-1850s): landed / aristocratic elites, provincial

1836 - Bangabhasha Prakashika Sabha

  • Formed by the associates of Raja Rammohan Roy; among the earliest.

1838 - Landholders' Society (Zamindari Association)

  • Founders: Dwarkanath Tagore and Raja Radhakant Deb.
  • First organised political effort; guarded zamindar (landlord) interests.

1843 - Bengal British India Society

  • On the advice of George Thompson; publish info on the people's real condition.

1851 - British India Association

  • Merger of the Landholders' Society and the Bengal British India Society (Pres.: Raja Radhakant Deb; Secy.: Debendranath Tagore).
  • 1852 petition to Parliament: separate legislature, separation of executive and judiciary, abolition of certain taxes.

1852 - Bombay Association

  • Founders: Jagannath Shankar Seth and Dadabhai Naoroji; ascertain the people's demands and present them to the British.

1852 - Madras Native Association

  • Gazulu Lakshminarasu Chetty; raised ryotwari peasant grievances; short-lived.

1852 - Deccan Association

  • Short-lived; tried to send a mission to England for Charter Act 1853 reforms, but failed.
Do not confuse the three "British India" bodies: British India Society = 1839, London, William Adam (also published the paper "British Indian Advocate") | Bengal British India Society = 1843, Calcutta | British Indian Association = 1851, the merger.
Phase 2 (1860s-1880s): more nationalist, broader-based

1865 - London Indian Society

  • Dadabhai Naoroji and W.C. Bonnerjee, in London; soon merged into the East India Association.

1866 - East India Association

  • Dadabhai Naoroji, in London; build British public opinion in favour of India's political progress.

1870 - Poona Sarvajanik Sabha

  • Founders: G.V. Joshi, S.H. Sathe, S. Chiplunkar (Ranade joined 1871; not a founder); later nurtured Gokhale and Tilak.
  • 1875 petition to the House of Commons for India's direct representation - signed by 23,000+ people.

1876 - Indian National Association

  • Surendranath Banerjee and Anand Mohan Bose; first avowedly nationalist body; led the Ilbert Bill and Civil Service age-limit agitations; merged into INC in 1886.

1878 - Mohammedan National Association

  • Founded by Syed Amir Ali.

1884 - Madras Mahajan Sabha

  • G. Subramania Iyer, P. Anandacharlu, M. Viraraghavachariar - forerunner of the Congress in the South.

1885 - Bombay Presidency Association

  • Pherozeshah Mehta, Badruddin Tyabji, K.T. Telang - the "Bombay Trio".

1893 - Indian Parliamentary Committee

  • William Wedderburn and W.S. Caine; gather support for Indian reform among the British public.
Demands (linked to the Charter Act 1853)
  • A legislature with a popular / representative character.
  • Separation of the executive from the judiciary.
  • Indianisation of services and reduction in the salaries of high officials.
  • Abolition of oppressive taxes - the salt duty and the stamp duty.
Assessment

They spread political consciousness, brought the masses into the political fold, critiqued British law and policy, and prepared a strong base for the Congress. Limitations: a narrow social base, a regional outlook and an elite bias - though they imbibed British democratic and constitutional ideas.

Part 3

Foundation of the INC (1885) and Theories of its Formation

Founded in December 1885 at Bombay; A.O. Hume was the prime mover and W.C. Bonnerjee the first president (72 delegates).

Safety-valve theory

The Congress was allegedly created as a "safety valve" to release discontent and prevent a revolt bigger than 1857. Advocates cite Hume's alleged "seven volumes" of secret reports at Simla and his acting with the knowledge of Viceroy Lord Dufferin.

Conspiracy theory - R.P. Dutt (India Today, 1940)

Building on the safety-valve idea: the Congress was the result of a conspiracy to abort a popular uprising, and the bourgeois leaders were complacent in it.

The extremists' use - Lala Lajpat Rai

Radicals turned the safety-valve claim against the Moderates - the Congress had been a device to protect British rule, so Indians must now fight for their rights.

Lightning-conductor theory - G.K. Gokhale

The Moderates deliberately kept A.O. Hume (white, ex-civil servant) in the lead as a shield, so the Government would not suppress the infant Congress; Hume was a lightning conductor to channel the nationalist forces.

Conscious-efforts view - accepted (Bipin Chandra)

Bipin Chandra debunked the "seven volumes" and safety-valve thesis as a myth. The Congress was the culmination of decades of conscious Indian effort; Hume was a facilitator, not the creator; the early leaders were patriots of high character, not stooges.

Part 4

The Moderates (Early Nationalists), 1885-1905

Who: Naoroji, Wacha, Pherozeshah Mehta, Surendranath Banerjee, Gokhale, Badruddin Tyabji, Ranade, W.C. Bonnerjee - English-educated professionals.

Ideology: faith in the British sense of justice and the "providential mission" of British rule (reformable from within); apprenticeship thought necessary; loyal to the Crown.

Methods: constitutional agitation; the "3 P's" - Petition, Prayer, Protest (some add a 4th P, Propaganda / political education); the British Committee of the INC (1889) and its journal "India"; Naoroji even became a British MP (1892). Derided later as "political mendicancy".

Demands: council expansion and more powers (budget, questions); Indianisation of services and simultaneous ICS exams; separation of judiciary and executive; and the economic "Drain of Wealth" critique - Naoroji (Poverty and Un-British Rule in India, 1901) and R.C. Dutt.

Achievements: the Indian Councils Act 1892; the Public Service Commission (1886); a House of Commons resolution (1893) for simultaneous ICS exams; the Welby Commission (1895); and exposing the economic character of British rule.

Evaluation: a narrow base and limited mass contact, and slow results - yet they laid the organisation, ideology and economic critique for later mass movements.

Part 5

The Extremists (Assertive Nationalists), 1905-1919

Causes of the rise:

  • Recognition of the exploitative nature of British rule - the drain, famines (1896-97, 1899-1900) and plague.
  • The slow, meagre results of Moderate methods.
  • Growth of self-respect and a militant school drawing pride from India's past.
  • Curzon's reactionary policies - Calcutta Corporation Act (1899), Indian Universities Act (1904), Official Secrets Act, and the Partition of Bengal (1905), the immediate trigger.
  • International influences - Ethiopia's defeat of Italy (1896) and Japan's defeat of Russia (1905).

Leaders and ideology: Lal-Bal-Pal, plus Aurobindo Ghosh and V.O.C. Pillai. Goal: Swaraj - "Swaraj is my birthright and I shall have it" (Tilak); drew on India's past and claimed rights as a matter of right.

Methods: Tilak's four-point programme - Swadeshi, Boycott, National Education, Passive Resistance; and mass mobilisation through the Ganpati Utsav (1893) and Shivaji Utsav (1896).

Surat Split (1907): the Congress split at Surat; reunited at Lucknow (1916). Under Extremist pressure the Moderates raised "No taxation without representation".

Tilak: "Political rights will have to be fought for. The Moderates think they can be won by persuasion; we think they can be obtained only by strong pressure."

Part 7

Indian Councils Act, 1861

  • Portfolio system: introduced by Canning (1858-59), recognised by this Act. Departments (1862): Military, Revenue, Finance, Home, Law; Public Works added 1874.
  • Additional (non-official) members: 6 to 12, nominated, 2-year term.
  • First Indians (nominated): the Raja of Banaras, the Maharaja of Patiala, Sir Dinkar Rao (by Canning, 1862).
  • Decentralisation begins: legislative powers restored to Bombay and Madras; later councils for Bengal (1862), N.W. Provinces (1886), Punjab (1897).
  • Ordinance: the Viceroy could issue ordinances valid for 6 months.
  • Limits: purely advisory - cannot discuss the budget, cannot ask questions, no control over the executive.

Significance: the beginning of representative institutions and of legislative decentralisation.

Part 8

Indian Councils Act, 1892

Executive CouncilNo change.
Additional Members (10 to 16)Official: 6. Non-official: 10 - of which 5 appointed by the Viceroy, 1 each from Bengal, Bombay, Madras, the N.W. Provinces and the Bengal Chamber of Commerce.
Principle of representationFirst indirect "election" - some members nominated on the recommendation of provincial councils, municipalities, universities and chambers of commerce. The word "election" was avoided (called "nomination on recommendation").
PowersCan discuss the budget (cannot vote). Can ask questions (no supplementary).
SignificanceThe first step towards a representative form of government.
Quick contrast: 1861 vs 1892
  • Budget: 1861 - cannot discuss; 1892 - can discuss (cannot vote).
  • Questions: 1861 - cannot ask; 1892 - can ask (no supplementary).
  • Members: 1861 - nomination; 1892 - nomination on recommendation.
  • Core idea: 1861 - Portfolio + Decentralisation; 1892 - Principle of Representation.
