Part 1

Regional Integration Theory

From the beginning of the 19th century there were many social-reform organisations that also worked for political awakening through newspapers, journals and books. They raised similar issues, which led to the formation of social organisations, then evolving into regional organisations (such as the Poona Sarvajanik Sabha), and ultimately culminating into a national-level organisation - the INC.

Part 2

Pre-Congress Political Associations

Early Bengal associations

1836 - Bangabhasha Prakashika Sabha

  • Formed by the associates of Raja Rammohan Roy.

1838 - Landholders' Society (Zamindari Association)

  • Founders: Dwarkanath Tagore and Raja Radhakant Deb.
  • Worked for the interests of the landlords (zamindars).

1851 - British India Association

  • Merger of the Landholders' Society and the Bengal British India Society.
  • Associated with Raja Radhakant Deb and Dwarkanath Tagore.
Later associations

1866 - East India Association

  • Formed by Dadabhai Naoroji in England.
  • Aim: build public opinion in favour of India's political progress.

1870 - Poona Sarvajanik Sabha

  • Founders: G.V. Joshi, S.H. Sathe, S. Chiplunkar (Ranade joined 1871; not a founder).
  • Later associated with Gokhale and Tilak.
  • 1875 petition to the House of Commons for India's direct representation in the British Parliament - signed by 23,000+ people.

Madras Mahajan Sabha (1884)

  • Founders: G. Subramania Iyer, P. Anandacharlu, M. Viraraghavachariar.

Bombay Presidency Association (1885)

  • Founders: Pherozeshah Mehta, Badruddin Tyabji, K.T. Telang - the "Bombay Trio".

1876 - Indian National Association

  • Founders: Anand Mohan Bose and Surendranath Banerjee.
  • Made demands to Parliament; aimed to grow into a national-level association.

1878 - Mohammedan National Association

  • Founded by Syed Amir Ali.
Other pre-Congress associations

1839 - British India Society

  • Founded by William Adam and George Thompson in England.
  • Aim: draw the attention of the British public to despotic rule in India.
  • Published a newspaper called "British Indian Advocate".

1843 - Bengal British India Society

  • Formed on the advice of George Thompson.

1852 - Bombay Association

  • Founders: Jagannath Shankar Seth and Dadabhai Naoroji.
  • Aim: ascertain the demands of the people and present them to the British.

1852 - Deccan Association

  • Short-lived; tried to send a mission to England for reforms under the Charter Act 1853, but failed.

1852 - Madras Native Association

  • Founded by Gazulu Lakshminarasu Chetty.
  • Raised the issue of peasants under the ryotwari settlement; criticised the reduction in public welfare.

1893 - Indian Parliamentary Committee

  • Formed by William Wedderburn and W.S. Caine.
  • Aim: gather support for Indian political reform among the British public.
Part 2 (contd.)

Demands and Assessment

Demands (petition to the British, linked to the Charter Act 1853):

  • Legislation with a popular character.
  • Separation of the executive and the judiciary.
  • Reduction in the salary of officials.
  • Abolition of the salt duty and the stamp duty.

Assessment: these bodies spread political consciousness, brought the common masses into the political fold, and critiqued British law and policy, preparing a strong base for the Congress. Their limitation was a narrow social base, a regional connotation and an elite bias - though they imbibed British democratic and constitutional ideas.

Part 3

Theories on the Formation of the INC

Conscious-efforts theory (most acceptable)

The formation of the INC was not a sudden event. There were many conscious efforts by Indians at various levels that culminated in the INC - the most acceptable view. Bipin Chandra supports it and called the early Congress leaders patriotic men of high character, in no way stooges of the Government.

Lightning-Conductor theory - G.K. Gokhale

Indian leaders knew that a platform openly led by Indian nationalists would be suppressed. So they kept A.O. Hume (white, an ex-civil servant) in the lead to shield the Congress. They used the Congress as a lightning conductor - a catalyst to bring together the nationalist forces, disguised as a safety wall.

Conspiracy theory - R.P. Dutt (India Today)

It builds on the safety-valve theory. According to him, the Congress was the result of a conspiracy to suppress a popular uprising in India, and the bourgeois leaders were complacent in it.

Part 5

Indian Councils Act, 1861

  • Portfolio system: introduced by Canning in 1858; recognised by the 1861 Act. Departments (1862): Military, Revenue, Finance, Home, Law; Public Works added in 1874.
  • Ordinance: the Viceroy could issue an ordinance valid for 6 months.
  • Decentralisation: the earlier over-centralisation was reversed - this Act marks the beginning of decentralisation in India.
  • Additional members (6 to 10): cannot ask questions to the Executive Council; cannot discuss the Budget; term of 2 years.
  • Categories: Official; and Non-official - Sir Dinkar Rao, the Raja of Banaras, the Maharaja of Patiala.
  • Gradually legislatures were set up in the N.W. Provinces, Punjab and Burma.
  • The Act gave the Viceroy the liberty to increase the members; the Governor-General's Council was expanded for law-making.

Indian Councils Act, 1892

Executive CouncilNo change.
Additional Members (10 to 16)Official: 6. Non-official: 10 - of which 5 appointed by the Viceroy, and 1 each from Bengal, Bombay, Madras, the N.W. Provinces and the Bengal Chamber of Commerce.
Powers of membersCan ask questions to the Executive Council. Can discuss financial matters (the Budget). Cannot vote on the Budget. Cannot ask supplementary questions.
SignificanceIntroduced the principle of representation; first step towards a representative form of government; first time indirect election - called not "election" but "nomination on the basis of recommendation by certain bodies".
