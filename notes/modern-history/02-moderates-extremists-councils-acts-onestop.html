Part 1

The Moderates (Early Nationalists), 1885-1905

Prominent leaders

Dadabhai Naoroji - Grand Old Man; Drain theory; first Indian MP S.N. Banerjee - Indian Association; "Rashtraguru" Pherozeshah Mehta - "Lion of Bombay" G.K. Gokhale - Servants of India Society; Gandhi's guru W.C. Bonnerjee - first Congress president Badruddin Tyabji - first Muslim president (1887) Wacha, Ranade, R.C. Dutt, Malaviya, Wedderburn

Ideology

Faith in the British sense of justice and in the "providential mission" of British rule (reformable from within); gradualism and constitutionalism; a period of political apprenticeship thought necessary; loyal to the Crown; shaped by Western liberal thought.

Methods

  • Constitutional agitation "within the four corners of the law".
  • The 3 P's - Petition, Prayer, Protest (some add a 4th P, Propaganda / political education).
  • A two-pronged strategy: build public opinion in India, and persuade Britain via the British Committee of the INC (1889) and its journal "India", and via Naoroji as an MP.
  • Derided by the Extremists as "political mendicancy" (politics of begging).
Their greatest contribution: Economic Nationalism (Drain of Wealth)

The Moderates built the most powerful economic critique of colonial rule - that British rule itself caused India's poverty.

  • Dadabhai Naoroji - "Poverty and Un-British Rule in India" (1901); the Drain theory.
  • R.C. Dutt - "The Economic History of India". William Digby - "Prosperous British India".
  • The drain: Home Charges, British salaries/pensions, interest on debt, guaranteed railway profits, military spending.
  • Three pillars: de-industrialisation; the drain of wealth; an exploitative land-revenue system.

Political and administrative demands

  • Expansion of the legislative councils, more elected Indians, and control over the budget.
  • Separation of the judiciary from the executive.
  • Indianisation of services - simultaneous ICS exams in India and England; raise the age limit.
  • Repeal of the Arms Act; civil liberties; more spending on education.

Achievements

  • Created a national awakening and trained Indians in modern politics.
  • The economic critique undermined the moral legitimacy of British rule (Bipan Chandra rates this highest).
  • The Indian Councils Act 1892; the Public Service Commission (1886); the Commons resolution (1893) for simultaneous ICS exams; the Welby Commission (1895, Naoroji a member).

Evaluation (Bipan Chandra)

Limitations: a narrow social base and little mass contact, an overestimation of British liberalism, and slow results. But they were pioneers, not failures - they laid the organisation, ideology and economic critique on which every later phase stood; their real work was the political education of a nation.

Part 2

The Extremists (Assertive Nationalists), 1905-1919

Causes of the rise of Extremism

  1. Realisation of the exploitative nature of British rule - the drain, famines (1896-97, 1899-1900), plague.
  2. Failure of the Moderate methods (petitions ignored; British hostility).
  3. Growth of self-respect and strong individual leaders (Tilak, Pal, Lajpat Rai, Aurobindo).
  4. Cultural pride and revival - Vivekananda, Dayananda, Bankim Chandra ("Anandamath", "Vande Mataram").
  5. Curzon's reactionary policies - Calcutta Corporation Act (1899), Indian Universities Act (1904), Official Secrets Act (1904), and the Partition of Bengal (1905) - the trigger.
  6. International influences - Ethiopia over Italy (1896), Japan over Russia (1905).
  7. An existing militant school of thought (Rajnarain Bose, Ashwini Kumar Dutt, Vasudev Balwant Phadke).

Leaders and ideology

Lala Lajpat Rai - Punjab Bal Gangadhar Tilak - Maharashtra Bipin Chandra Pal - Bengal Aurobindo Ghosh V.O.C. Pillai

Goal: Swaraj - "Swaraj is my birthright and I shall have it" (Tilak). Pride in India's past; self-reliance; deep faith in the masses; rights claimed as a matter of right.

Methods - the four-fold programme

Swadeshi | Boycott | National Education | Passive Resistance

Plus mass mobilisation through Tilak's Ganpati Utsav (1893) and Shivaji Utsav (1896), and newspapers (Kesari, Maratha). National education, e.g., the Bengal National College (1906), with Aurobindo as principal.

The Swadeshi Movement (1905-1908)

  • Launched in Bengal against the Partition of Bengal (1905).
  • Forms: boycott and burning of foreign cloth; swadeshi enterprises; national education; cultural mobilisation (Tagore's Rakhi Bandhan, "Vande Mataram").
  • The Extremists wanted it spread nationwide and turned into a movement for Swaraj.
  • Limits: limited peasant participation, splits, and severe repression; declined after 1908.

The Surat Split (1907) and repression

  • At Calcutta (1906), the Congress adopted the four resolutions and used "Swaraj" as its goal for the first time (Naoroji presided).
  • At Surat (1907), the Congress split - Moderates (Gokhale) vs Extremists (Tilak); the Extremists walked out.
  • Repression: Tilak jailed 6 years in Mandalay (1908); Aurobindo and Pal withdrew; Lajpat Rai left abroad.
  • The two wings reunited at Lucknow (1916).

Part 4

Indian Councils Act, 1861 Portfolio + Decentralisation

Context: after 1857 and Crown rule (1858), Britain sought to associate Indians with law-making and reverse the over-centralisation of the Charter Act 1833.

  • Portfolio system recognised (introduced by Canning, 1859). Departments (1862): Military, Revenue, Finance, Home, Law; Public Works added 1874.
  • Additional (non-official) members for legislation: 6 to 12, nominated, 2-year term.
  • First Indians (nominated): the Raja of Banaras, the Maharaja of Patiala, Sir Dinkar Rao (by Canning, 1862).
  • Decentralisation begins: legislative powers restored to Bombay and Madras; later councils for Bengal (1862), N.W. Provinces (1886), Punjab (1897).
  • Ordinance: the Viceroy could issue ordinances valid for 6 months.
  • Limits: purely advisory - cannot discuss the budget, cannot ask questions, no control over the executive; members nominated, not elected.

Significance: the beginning of representative institutions and of legislative decentralisation.

Part 5

Indian Councils Act, 1892 Representation + Budget

Executive CouncilNo change.
Additional Members (10 to 16)Official: 6. Non-official: 10 - of which 5 appointed by the Viceroy, and 1 each from Bengal, Bombay, Madras, the N.W. Provinces and the Bengal Chamber of Commerce.
Principle of representationFirst indirect "election" - members nominated on the recommendation of provincial councils, municipalities, universities and chambers of commerce. The word "election" was avoided ("nomination on recommendation").
Powers of membersCan discuss the budget (cannot vote). Can ask questions (no supplementary).
SignificanceThe first step towards a representative form of government.

Quick contrast: 1861 vs 1892

  • Budget: 1861 - cannot discuss; 1892 - can discuss (cannot vote).
  • Questions: 1861 - cannot ask; 1892 - can ask (no supplementary).
  • Members: 1861 - nomination; 1892 - nomination on recommendation.
  • Core idea: 1861 - Portfolio + Decentralisation; 1892 - Principle of Representation.
